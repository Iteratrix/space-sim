# 06 — Maritime and sea-nomad cultures

Status: complete first pass (2026-09-06). Sections per BRIEF.md.

Framing used throughout: each finding is stated as an **inverted settled assumption** (what a land-based sim would take for granted, and what the maritime record says instead), with the evidence, and then picked up again in section 3 as a mechanic with an act.

## 1. Findings

### 1.1 Polynesian / Austronesian expansion: voyaging is windowed and capital-locked

**Inverted assumption: movement is continuous and cheap. Maritime record: movement happens in windows, and between windows the society is effectively sedentary.**

- The Austronesian expansion is not a steady diffusion. West Polynesia (Tonga, Samoa) was reached by ~900 BC; then there is a **"long pause" of roughly 1,700 years** before East Polynesia was settled in a burst between ~AD 900 and 1250 (National Geographic summary of the archaeology). Explanations offered: multidecadal drought "shocks" in the west and lusher conditions in the east ~1,000 years ago, population growth in Samoa, and canoe/rig advances allowing upwind sailing.
- Goodwin et al. 2014 (PNAS) reconstructed paleowind fields at bidecadal resolution and found that the routes to New Zealand and Easter Island lay **downwind** during the known colonization intervals; intensification and poleward expansion of the subtropical anticyclone culminating in **AD 1140–1260** opened an "anomalous climate window" during which off-wind sailing reached the far corners. The point for the sim: the same fleet and the same skills produce zero reach in one century and continental reach in the next, purely because of the environment's schedule. Exploration is not a tech unlock; it is a coincidence of capability and window.
- Every maritime economy in this report runs on a calendar imposed from outside: the Makassan trepang fleet left Sulawesi on the north-west monsoon (December/January), fished Arnhem Land for ~4 months and went home on the south-east monsoon (April); the Moken live afloat in the dry north-east monsoon (Nov–Apr) and on land May–Oct; the Icelandic Althing met for **two weeks in June** because that is when travel was possible and there was daylight. Governance, trade and marriage markets all cluster at the window (Byock: the Althing was where "young adults advertising for spouses" converged).

**Inverted assumption: exploration is driven by curiosity or resource surplus. Record: it is driven by primogeniture and population pressure, and led by those who lose the inheritance lottery.**

- Proto-Oceanic society had ranked descent groups with primogeniture; the conical clan "has the inherent tendency to decentralize into independent segments because of primogeniture pushing out younger sons." Colonizing expeditions were "probably led by junior, polygynous collaterals of chiefs." Settlement fissioning is a social relief valve: the group that leaves is defined by rank, not by volunteering.

### 1.2 Wayfinding knowledge as the cultural technology

**Inverted assumption: technology is embodied in tools and can be copied. Record: the decisive capital is in a handful of heads, is deliberately kept there, and can be lost in one generation.**

- Carolinian training starts at age 5–8 (star names) and a navigator is not ready to command before 18–20; pwo initiation typically follows 7–8 years of private instruction. Knowledge was "handed down only to a select few," within families and named schools (Weriyeng, Fanur), with five ranked grades above pwo. Chants are "cryptic in content, requiring commentary"; islands have secret synonyms so navigators can "discuss voyages within the hearing of others without being understood." When a teacher dies before passing the commentary, students "develop their own interpretation" — knowledge mutates on transmission.
- The loss is documented, not hypothetical. On Pollap (the traditional teaching centre) the practice disappeared because parents died before teaching children. Mau Piailug was the **last pwo initiated on Satawal in 1951**; the next ceremony there was 2007 (16 initiates, 11 Satawalese and 5 Hawaiians). Mau broke the secrecy rule deliberately, teaching outsiders (Hōkūleʻa, 1976) to "send a message to his own people that navigation is important." The Marshallese ri-meto were likewise trained in secrecy, blindfolded on the water to learn swell patterns by feel; a candidate had to pass a chief-devised voyaging test "on the first try."
- Status: "on land the power of navigators almost rivals that of the chiefs — and at sea, the navigator is supreme." A new pwo assumes a quasi-chiefly position with obligations to voyage for the community. Navigators observe taboos (no sex before and during a voyage until safe return, separately prepared food, the canoe house as their preserve) and double as ritual specialists (storm spells, amulets, "special rhetoric and spells associated with politics and diplomacy").
- Method: etak dead reckoning (a reference island imagined moving past the canoe; the "etak of birds" ≈ 18 mi), a 32-point star compass, swell reading. Stick charts (rebbelib) are teaching devices, not carried at sea. Two features matter for the sim: the knowledge is *procedural and tacit* (the ri-meto lies in the hull to feel the swell) and it is *ship-independent* (Mau navigated a Hawaiian-built canoe).

### 1.3 Why voyaging stopped: the default is drift toward isolation

**Inverted assumption: once you can reach a place, you keep being able to. Record: reach decays unless exercised and fed; isolation is the attractor.**

- Rapa Nui: an estimated **15–19.7 million palms** destroyed between ~1200 and 1650 (palms took ~70 years to mature). By 1722 the Dutch found canoes "made of small, narrow pieces of wood glued together... so leaky that the islanders spent half their time bailing"; Cook called them the worst canoes in the Pacific. Voyaging did not stop by decision; the substrate for the capital asset disappeared.
- Mangareva (max elevation 441 m, one quarter of Moʻorea's) had only one of the two hull timbers (*Calophyllum inophyllum* / tamanu; no *Neonauclea* / mara), so there was no resource-switching when swidden erosion (~950–1400 CE) hit the lowland tamanu. The Frontiers 2021 multiproxy study concludes deforestation on Mangareva "likely serv[ed] as one cause leading to the abandonment of Pitcairn and Henderson islands" — **an outpost dies because a different island lost its shipyard**. By contact, Gambier and Rapa Nui "retained only small single-hulled canoes or bark rafts."
- New Zealand: settled ~1250–1300; "no evidence to suggest that contact was maintained between Māori and their tropical homeland." Isolation, size and resource abundance removed the motive; the temperate forest removed the familiar materials. Coastal capability persisted at a shorter range (Chathams settled 1300–1550, 750 km). The reach shrank to the scale that daily life demanded.
- Greenland (touched lightly; another report covers cut-off outposts): population 2,000–3,000, ~500 farms in the Eastern Settlement, entirely dependent on imported iron and timber (a Greenland ship drifted off course "while sailing to Markland for wood" in 1347). Western Settlement abandoned ~1350; last written record 1408 (a wedding at Hvalsey), last ship home 1410, last radiocarbon date 1430±15. Walrus ivory lost its market when elephant ivory returned to Europe. The outpost died when the trade good stopped paying for the ship.

### 1.4 Chiefdoms, mana, tapu, and the canoe as the community's largest capital project

**Inverted assumption: the biggest thing a settlement builds is a building. Record: it is a hull, and building one reorganizes politics, religion and labor.**

- Society Islands high chiefs (ariʻi nui) "strictly controlled ocean-going canoe production"; building happened "on temple grounds... under formalized rules." Two builder classes: public canoe-builders (tahuʻa tarai vaʻa) and sacred builders (ahi-tu) who "lived consecrated lives and would not cut their hair until a canoe was complete." In the archaeoecology database, construction accounts for **53% of all recorded canoe life-cycle activities**.
- Scale: pāhi up to 30+ m double-hulled; war canoes 32+ m with 144 paddlers; ʻarioi flotillas of "60–70 canoes carrying some 700 persons." A Taumako tepuke needs ~**1,500 m of sennit cordage**; the whole community rotates between building and feeding the builders; leaders "planted gardens to feed workers." Māori tohunga selected the tree and lifted tapu; only men of rank worked a war canoe.
- Provisioning: Hōkūleʻa (62 ft, 12.5 t displacement, ~5.5 t payload including crew) carried 12–15 crew and about a ton of food; modern allotment 1 quart of water per person per day; Hawaiʻi→Tahiti 33 days, return 22. Traditional stores were dried/fermented (fermented breadfruit keeps ~10 years) plus pigs, chickens, dogs and ~two dozen plant species as founder stock.
- Resource regulation: rāhui is "a form of tapu applied to a class of resources or to a territory," imposed by a chief and "given its authority by the mana of the person or group that imposes it." Reefs were closed for years then opened for feasts or chiefly marriages; rāhui also follow a death or drowning (the area becomes tapu). No formal penalties — enforcement is reputational and religious. Mana is a depletable, transferable authority stat that resource closures both spend and demonstrate.

### 1.5 Sea nomads: Sama-Bajau, Moken, Orang Laut — testing the "closest analog" claim

**Inverted assumption: the mobile group is autonomous. Record: sea nomads are the most dependent people in their region, and are treated as property by the people who cannot do without them.**

- Sama Dilaut / Bajau Laut are "entirely non-agricultural, without crops or landed property"; "cultivated foodstuffs are obtained entirely by trade." Boat-nomad communities owned no land at all except "small burial islands." They supplied patrons with pearls, mother-of-pearl, trepang, tortoise-shell and dried fish "in return for assurances of physical security, a moorage site, and agricultural foodstuffs." The Sulu sultan "claimed proprietary rights over all boat-dwelling Bajau"; shore patrons spoke of "'their' boat-nomadic clients as 'property'" — but the claim was hollow because clients "were able to move relatively easily from the territorial sphere of one leader to another." Lacking territory, the Bajau Laut "were without formal jural status in the Sulu Sultanate," stigmatized as *luwaan* ("spat out"), and placed at the bottom of a stratification that fused rank, religion and allegiance.
- Household = boat: one nuclear family plus one or two kin, 5–6 people, in a ~10 m plank boat with hearth and roof. Two to six boats fish and anchor together as a *pagmundah*, pooling nets and food; several *pagmundah* share a moorage (*sambuangan*). Marriage: bride-wealth; couples may sail with either spouse's kin (residence is a choice, mildly uxorilocal); independent household within 2–3 years; divorce frequent early, rare later. Kinship bilateral and shallow (kindred to shared grandparents). Death rites are Islamic with commemorations at 20, 40, 100 days and one year; during epidemics a spirit boat is loaded and sent to sea. Leadership: no parish organization, "no formal representation in the state except through their patrons."
- Physiology: Ilardo et al. 2018 (Cell) measured median spleen ~**175 cm³ in Bajau vs ~100 cm³ in neighbouring Saluan** (≈50% larger), present in non-diving Bajau too, associated with selection on PDE10A — a heritable, population-level adaptation to the occupation, not training.
- Collapse of the niche: "With the dissolution of the Sulu Sultanate, and the breakdown of procurement trade... the specialized adaptation of the Bajau Laut also broke down," giving "an almost complete disappearance of boat nomadism" in the 20th century. Money-based fish markets replaced seasonal barter at moorages and pulled people ashore. Boat-dwellers now number "fewer than 10,000" of ~1.3 million Sama-Bajau. Sabah evicted ~500 stateless Bajau Laut in June 2024 citing border security, illegal fishing and structures in a marine park; Malaysia and Indonesia run resettlement programs with agricultural incentives. Moken (2,000–3,000; 200–300 on Surin) were confined to one government-built village after the 2004 tsunami destroyed most kabang; the last kabang there was built in 2006.
- Orang Laut show the other branch. They were the navy and message service of Srivijaya, Melaka and Johor: "gather sea products for the China trade... serve as transport for envoys and royal missives, man the ships and serve as a fighting force on the ruler's fleet, and patrol the waters." They were ranked into *suku* (Galang as fighters; Mantang tending the ruler's hunting dogs). When "central hegemony" broke, fighting suku "transferred allegiance to local Malay chieftains as pirate crews"; piracy suppression in the mid-19th century forced rapid sedentarization; high-status suku assimilated into the Malay population, low-status ones stayed a separate ethnicity.

**Verdict on the brief's hypothesis.** The Bajau are the best analog for the *social position* of a belt population — mobile, physiologically adapted, indispensable to and despised by their patrons, stateless, and dissolved the moment their trade good stopped paying. They are a poor analog for the *economics of movement*: their boats are cheap family assets built by other people (Sama Kubang boatwrights), their moves are short, cheap and unwindowed, and their skills are universal rather than secret. The belt combines Bajau politics with Carolinian knowledge economics and whaling-voyage logistics. No single society is the analog; see 1.9.

### 1.6 Norse Atlantic: the ship as institution, the Althing, Greenland

**Inverted assumption: government needs an executive. Record: a scattered, oceanic society ran ~330 years of law with no executive, no taxes for defense, and private enforcement.**

- Iceland, ~930–1262/64. Byock: "no executive powers were given to anybody." ~36 chieftaincies (goðorð) initially, 39 fixed in the 960s reforms, 48 goðar after balancing seats; each goðorð could be shared, so goðar outnumbered goðorð. A goðorð was property — bought, sold, inherited, shared. A thingman chose his goði and "could transfer his allegiance (and attendant fees) at will from one Chieftain to another without moving." The lögrétta legislated; a lawspeaker elected for three years recited a third of the law each year from memory at the Law Rock. Once judgment was given, "criminal acts were regarded as private concerns to be settled between the injured and the offending parties or their advocates" — professionals who "contracted out their services"; the right to prosecute could be bought from a kinsman. Penalty was compensation or outlawry. "Many, perhaps even a majority" of disputes were arbitrated, using the courts as a price reference. Failure mode: by ~1220 a "loose federation of 10–12 regional powers" (Sturlung era), then submission to Norway. Byock's own summary: the system worked because "recourse to violence was in most instances more costly than using the services of advocates and arbitrators."
- The ship as institution: *félag* ("laying property together") was the Norse joint venture — each partner deposited goods or labor and took a share of profit; Grágás required surviving partners to protect the rights of a dead partner's heirs. The same hull did raiding, trading and settling depending on what the season offered, and the Orang Laut history (1.5) shows the same crew turning to piracy when the patron fell. Raid/trade/settle is one continuum selected by opportunity, not three professions.

### 1.7 Ship-as-society: whalers, the lay system, Bugis/Makassans, the Hansa

**Inverted assumption: pay is periodic and labor is a wage. Record: on a multi-year voyage there is no pay until return, everyone holds equity, and the owner takes most of it.**

- American whaling, 1816–1906 (Davis, Gallman & Gleiter; NHA; Costello). Voyages 2–4 years, up to 5 (Pacific averages "passing three years and in the latter decades nearing four"). A four-year voyage begun at 20 was about one-fifth of a man's remaining life expectancy. Lays (fractions of net proceeds): captain ~1/12–1/16 (Davis et al. put the mid-century norm at ~1/15), first mate ~1/18–1/25 (1/23), second/third mates 1/38–1/65, boatsteerer ~1/80–1/110 (1/87), cooper 1/30–1/65 because the skill was scarce, seamen 1/120–1/180, greenhands 1/160–1/220, cabin boy to 1/350. Melville shipped at 1/175. Owners and agents kept 60–70%. The whaling agent "functioned as both raiser and allocator of capital" and picked the captain and mates. Advances (~$75) carried 25% interest; slop-chest goods were marked up; officers paid 40% to remit money home; desertion forfeited the whole lay, and agents sometimes wanted the return leg made unbearable "to provoke desertion." Typical net: captain ~$2,000 for three years, seaman ~$200, often less than nothing. Desertion ran ~60% per voyage by the late 19th century even on the Charles W. Morgan (37 voyages, 9 months to 5 years each, average crew 33, >1,000 men over her life, $1.4 M lifetime returns). Captains' families sailed with them.
- The shore society is the inverse image. On Nantucket, Centre Street was "Petticoat Row" because the shopkeepers were wives and widows; women acted as "she-merchants," kept the account books, bought and sold property, ran farms and inns. By age 24 over a quarter of whaling wives had been widowed by the sea. The polity that the voyager returns to is not the one he left, and it is run by the people who stayed.
- Makassar → Marege (Arnhem Land), 1720s–1907: 30–60 prahu and ~1,000 men each season, ~30 (up to 50) per prahu, ~2,000 km in 10–15 days on the monsoon, four months on the coast, Chinese merchants in Makassar buying the trepang. The Yolngu built a whole relational infrastructure around an annual visitor: colour-coded flags marking which clan's beach a crew could land on, loanwords in the hundreds, Yolngu men shipping to Makassar and Singapore and founding mixed families, Makassan figures entering Yolngu ceremony. It ended by administrative act — licence taxes from 1884 and a final voyage in 1906–07 — not by any failure of the sea. Bugis society, which supplied much of the crew, is built on *siri'* (honor/shame), a three-tier rank system with an "ethos of enterprise" allowing mobility, and *passompe* ("to sail") — emigration as the normal career after the fall of Makassar in 1669.
- The Hanseatic League, 1356–1669: a confederation of dozens of towns with "no permanent administrative body, treasury, or standing military." The Diet (Hansetag) met irregularly in Lübeck, decisions required no objection (*Einung*), attendance was poor, and the last Diet (1669) drew "a few towns" 40 years after the previous one. Its only sanctions were exclusion (*Verhansung*) and collective boycott: the 1358 Bruges blockade required merchants to withdraw entirely and present certificates of non-compliance, with "permanent exclusion from Hansa privileges" for defectors; Christian Kelmer was barred from the London kontor in 1385 for paying a duty the Hansa contested. Kontors (London Steelyard, Bruges, Bergen, Novgorod) were extraterritorial enclaves with elected aldermen — voters could only choose candidates born in a *different* Hansa region, to break factions. Decline: Dutch price competition, silting of Bruges, Ivan III closing Novgorod (1494, 49 merchants jailed), new trade corridors.
- Ship as fractional property: Dutch *partenrederij* / German *Partenreederei* divided a hull into 16, 32 or 64 shares; investors held single shares in many hulls. The asset is a portfolio position, not a homestead.

### 1.8 Cross-cutting: property, the missing, the dead, departure and return

- **Property in vessels.** Three regimes appear: (a) family-owned small hull as household (Bajau, Moken) — inherited within the kindred, residence choice follows the hull; (b) community/chiefly hull built by ritual specialists and controlled by the chief (Polynesia) — nobody "owns" it, the chief commands it, the community feeds its construction; (c) fractional commercial ownership (félag, partenrederij, whaling agents) — voyages funded by agents, crew paid in equity. The belt fleet will move along this axis across the acts: (c) under a sponsor, (b) when self-built in act 2, then a mix in act 3.
- **The missing.** Common law presumed death after seven years' silence; maritime jurisdictions shorten it after a shipwreck (six months in Germany, Austria, Poland). Chinese law restores a marriage when a declared-dead absentee returns unless the spouse has remarried. Grágás obliged félag survivors to protect a dead partner's heirs. Rāhui is placed on an area after a drowning. Nantucket's widows' walks and Petticoat Row are the institutional response to chronic uncertainty: authority passes to the stay-behinds and does not automatically pass back.
- **The dead.** Sea burial developed a "hybrid ceremony" reproducing land custom with sea-specific motives; Bajau bury on the only land they own; Moken erect spirit poles for ancestors; Yap outer islanders attributed typhoons to Yapese sorcery for unpaid tribute. Every maritime culture in this report treats a lost hull as an event requiring a closure (rāhui), a ritual, and a re-allocation of obligations.
- **Departure and return.** A new hull is never launched unblessed (kahuna, seawater, ti leaves, offerings at the bow); Samoan ʻava before a voyage; navigator taboos begin at departure and end at safe return; a returning Hōkūleʻa crew is met with lei, a circle of chants and a spear-throwing rite. Functionally these are checklists, status transitions and public commitment devices: the navigator's abstinence marks him as "in voyage" and separates him from land politics.
- **Tribute as insurance.** The Yap *sawei*: 24 coral islands sent annual canoe tribute (mats, cloth, coconut oil) to Gagil estates and received back yams, turmeric, pots, bamboo, red soil and foreign goods "of great importance... especially in times of post-typhoon shortages." Rank "decreased with distance from Yap"; the ideology was landlord–serf and parent–child, with no intermarriage and mandatory deference; non-payment was punished by sorcery-storms; the network also let outer islands call on each other after disasters. The Japanese ended it in the 1920s by banning long-distance canoe voyages. This is a hub-and-spoke confederation held together by the periphery's need for materials only the hub has and by an insurance pool.

### 1.9 Where the maritime analogy fits the belt better than the steppe, and where it does not

Fits better than steppe:
1. **Cost and windows.** Steppe movement is cheap, continuous, and the whole household goes with the herd. Belt transit is a capital event on a launch-window calendar; that is the Polynesian/Makassan/Althing pattern exactly.
2. **The vessel as the biggest capital asset that does not reproduce itself.** Herds breed; canoes and whaleships are built from depletable stock (tamanu, palms, timber for Greenland, feedstock in the belt) and rot. Loss of the substrate ends reach (1.3).
3. **Knowledge as capital held by lineages** (navigators, coopers, boatwrights) that can die out in one generation, with an explicit secrecy-vs-teaching dilemma (1.2).
4. **Two-tier society**: a voyaging minority under ship discipline and equity pay, and a shore majority that governs in their absence (1.7).
5. **Client status of the mobile specialists** toward staple-producing patrons, and sedentarization pressure when the trade good loses value or markets monetize (1.5).
6. **Governance without executive** across scattered nodes with exclusion as the only sanction (1.6, Hansa).

Fits worse than steppe or fits neither:
1. **The road is empty.** For sea nomads the road is also the field: fish on the way, rain to catch. Belt transits provide nothing en route; the vessel must be closed. The nearest maritime case is the whaleship (which fed itself but sailed to a resource, not through it), and whaling's 2–4-year voyage durations are a better fit than Polynesia's 20–40-day passages. Time-away mechanics should be calibrated on whaling, not on canoes.
2. **Windows are computable.** Trade winds and monsoons are statistical; orbital windows are deterministic. So the navigator's tacit skill maps less onto "reading the swell" and more onto ship-specific engineering knowledge, maintenance lore, and the judgment of which window to take with a marginal hull. Secrecy in the belt attaches to tacit maintenance knowledge and to trajectory software/hardware, not to sensing.
3. **No raid economy from the sea.** Vikings and Orang Laut turned to raiding cheaply because the same hull could do it. In the belt, piracy is delta-v-expensive and the target is a moving hull; the steppe's "raid the settled" model and the Norse continuum both need care. The Orang Laut case (naval clients becoming pirates when the patron collapses) is still the best template for *who* turns to it.
4. **Physiological adaptation** exists in both (spleen), but the belt version is likely a one-way door (bone, cardiovascular, radiation exposure) rather than an occupational advantage; the Bajau can move ashore, and did.
5. **Steppe still wins** for: state–nomad tax dynamics with a large mobile population, wealth that reproduces (herds), and confederation by charismatic leadership. Maritime confederations (Hansa, Althing, sawei) are confederations of *nodes*, which is the act-3 shape.

## 3. Implications for mechanics

Each entry: inverted assumption → mechanic → evidence → act.

1. **Movement is windowed, so the turn is the window.** Make the fundamental turn the launch window between the outpost and its trading partners, not a fixed month. Between windows the settlement is sedentary: politics, marriage, disputes, and the assembly (below) all resolve at the window. Evidence: monsoon fleets, the June Althing, the AD 1140–1260 climate window. Act 1 onward; it is the spine of the calendar.

2. **Reach is a stat that decays.** Give each settlement a "Reach" value (the farthest window it can actually take, in delta-v/time) that falls when no hull is built or refitted for N windows, when a knowledge-holder dies untaught, or when hull feedstock is depleted. The default trajectory is toward isolation; the player must spend to keep it. Evidence: Rapa Nui and Mangareva losing hull timber; Pitcairn/Henderson dying when Mangareva's shipyard failed; NZ never returning; Greenland when ships stopped. Act 2's central threat is not only cultural drift but *losing the ability to leave*. In act 3, a settlement's Reach determines whether it is a node or a client.

3. **Knowledge holders are named, countable, and mortal.** Model critical tacit skills (pilot, hull-wright, life-support engineer, the belt's cooper) as attributes of individual people with a training pipeline of 7–8 years, a minimum age, and a "school" lineage. A holder who dies with no initiated student drops the settlement's capability by a step regardless of what documentation exists. Offer the Mau Piailug choice: teach outsiders (or another faction) to preserve the skill at the cost of the lineage's monopoly and prestige. Evidence: pwo gap 1951–2007; Pollap losing the art; secret island names. Act 2 seed, payoff in act 3 when your pilots are the confederation's asset.

4. **The hull is the capital project, and building one is a political and religious act.** The first self-built long-range hull should cost multiple windows of community surplus, require a consecrated specialist group with its own rules (the ahi-tu who do not cut their hair), and be commanded by the chief, not owned by anyone. Track feedstock as depletable. Launch requires a ritual event; skipping it costs cohesion. Evidence: 53% of canoe life-cycle activity is construction; 1,500 m of sennit; leaders planting gardens to feed builders. Act 2 capstone; act 1 has it in embryo as sponsor-owned refits.

5. **Pay is equity, settled at return.** For any crewed voyage, compensation is a lay: a fraction of net proceeds paid on return, with the owner/sponsor taking 60–70% and the fraction scaled by rank and skill scarcity. Advances create debt; desertion (defection to another settlement mid-voyage) forfeits the lay. The lay schedule is a political dial: too long lays cause desertion (60% historical), too short cause the sponsor to withhold refits. Evidence: whaling lays, slop chest, agents provoking desertion. Act 1: the sponsor's contract *is* a lay and the advisor ring argues about the fractions. Act 2: the settlement runs its own lays and discovers the same fights internally.

6. **Two polities: Hull and Rock.** Split authority between the crew away (ship discipline, captain supreme, navigator supreme at sea) and the stay-behinds (who run the economy, property, and marriage market while the voyage is out). When a crew returns after a multi-window voyage, offices and property may have been reassigned; a returning captain does not automatically resume rank. Evidence: Petticoat Row, she-merchants, a quarter of wives widowed by 24. Act 2, when voyages become long enough for this to bite; seeds in act 1 with sponsor crews rotating through.

7. **The overdue state.** A hull that misses its window enters "overdue": neither dead nor alive. Its crew's shares are frozen, spouses cannot remarry, offices are held by deputies, and a rāhui-style closure (reduced extraction, ritual spend) applies to the sector it was working. After a culture-set period (7 years common law; 6 months if wreckage is confirmed) the estate is settled and the closure lifts. If the crew then returns, the Chinese-law rule applies: marriages restore unless the spouse remarried. Evidence: presumption-of-death rules, félag heir protections, rāhui after drowning. Act 1 onward; a cheap, high-drama mechanic.

8. **Patron–client, not sovereignty.** In act 1 the outpost is a moorage group and the sponsor is the Tausug patron: it supplies staples the outpost cannot make (pharma, semiconductors, seed stock) and "protection," calls the outpost its property, and cannot actually enforce that claim if the outpost can move. The client's leverage is exactly the ability to shift to another patron. Model the sponsor's demand to fix the outpost's position, register its people, and monetize its output as *sedentarization pressure*, rising whenever the trade good (volatiles) loses value or a cash market replaces barter. Evidence: Sulu sultanate's proprietary claim, Bajau clients relocating, sedentarization when trepang and pearl trade collapsed, 2024 evictions. Act 1 core tension; act 3 replays it with the inner system as the returning patron.

9. **Which suku are you.** Seed a rank choice early: does the outpost become the patron's navy/communications arm (Orang Laut Galang; Bugis mercenaries — high status, assimilated later) or the despised harvester (Bajau Laut, luwaan — low status, culturally durable)? High-status clients gain titles and inputs and lose distinctiveness; low-status clients stay separate and stateless. When the patron collapses (act 2 start), the naval clients turn to raiding under local strongmen; the harvesters simply move. Evidence: Orang Laut suku history; Sama assimilation gradient. Act 1 seed, act 2 consequence.

10. **Assembly without executive.** The act-3 confederation is an Althing: an assembly at the common window, chieftaincies as transferable offices that followers can defect from without moving, a lawspeaker whose only power is memory and recitation, judgments enforced privately (compensation, outlawry = loss of docking and refuel rights), and most disputes arbitrated against the court's price. Its only collective sanction is exclusion (Verhansung) and boycott, decisions need no objection, and attendance is a variable the player must manage. Failure mode: consolidation into 10–12 blocs and a call for an outside king (the inner system). Evidence: Byock; Hansa Diet 1356–1669; Kelmer barred 1385. Act 3.

11. **Tribute as insurance, rank by distance.** Alternative act-3 shape: a hub-and-spoke sawei in which peripheral nodes send annual tribute to the node holding the scarce input (the one shipyard, the one pharma line) and receive materials plus a claim on disaster relief; rank falls with distance from the hub; non-payment is punished by withholding and by the hub's monopoly on "magic" (the thing only it can make). Evidence: Yap sawei, 24 islands, ended only by an outside power banning voyages. Act 3; contrast with mechanic 10 as the player's choice between a flat Althing and a ranked sawei.

12. **Resource closures spend and prove mana.** Implement rāhui: a leader can close a body or sector to extraction for a set period; compliance depends on the leader's mana (authority stat); a successfully held closure raises mana and regenerates the resource; a violated closure costs mana with no formal penalty available. Closures are also placed after deaths. Evidence: rāhui, tapu, chiefly reef closures opened for feasts. Act 1 onward; ties leadership legitimacy to ecology.

13. **Annual visitors build creoles.** Any recurring seasonal contact with another culture (sponsor ships in act 1, other groups in act 2, inner-system returns in act 3) should accrete: loanwords, intermarriage, people leaving with the visitors, flags/landing rights per clan, and the visitors' figures entering local ritual. Contact ends by administrative act (taxes, licences) rather than by loss of capability. Evidence: Makassan–Yolngu 1720s–1907, ended by 1884 licence fees. Cultural-drift engine for act 2 with seeds in act 1.

14. **Who leaves.** When population exceeds what the outpost's staple supply supports, fission is led by junior lineages, not volunteers. Model rank inheritance (primogeniture or otherwise) so that the group that founds the next outpost is determined by who lost the inheritance lottery; they take a hull, founder stock, and a navigator. Evidence: Lapita/Polynesian expansion led by "junior, polygynous collaterals of chiefs." Act 2–3; this is how the belt gets populated with your own descendants who are now rivals.

15. **Adaptation is a one-way door.** Physiological adaptation to the belt (the belt's spleen) raises harvesting yield and voyage tolerance but reduces the option of returning to a patron's gravity well; the Bajau went ashore, the Voidborn may not be able to. Make adaptation a slow population trait with real yield benefits and a rising cost to reintegration in act 3. Evidence: heritable spleen enlargement; Moken confined ashore losing the kabang within two years. Act 2 drift, act 3 leverage and constraint.

16. **The navigator is separate from land politics while in voyage.** Voyaging crews should be removed from the political sim between departure and return rituals: they cannot vote, marry, or be prosecuted; their taboo state is the mechanism. This keeps the two-polity model (mechanic 6) simple and gives departure/return events a function beyond flavor. Evidence: navigator taboos, the canoe house as preserve, return rites. Act 1 onward.

## 4. Open questions

- Voyage durations in the belt are whaling-scale (years), not canoe-scale (weeks). Does the design want the two-polity split (mechanic 6) from act 1, or only after voyages exceed some threshold? The whaling record says the split appears once absences exceed ~1 year.
- The Bajau analogy for act 1 makes the *whole outpost* the client. In act 2–3 the outpost itself has a mobile minority and a sedentary majority. Which group inherits the "sea people" position — and does the sim let the hab treat its own fleet crews as luwaan?
- Grágás rules for a partner or heir lost at sea could not be retrieved (search budget exhausted); the félag heir-protection clause is known but the waiting period is not. Needed to calibrate the overdue state.
- No quantitative loss rate for Carolinian or Polynesian voyages was found (canoes lost per voyage). Whaling mortality and desertion are documented; if the sim wants a per-voyage loss probability, whaling and the Norse Greenland record (ship traffic ceasing over decades) are the available anchors.
- The Frontiers paper's "7–8 years to maturity" for tamanu is an ecological figure, not a hull-timber figure; the time to a hull-grade trunk is likely far longer (Rapa Nui palms ~70 years). Feedstock regeneration time is a design choice.
- How strongly should knowledge secrecy be a *player* choice versus an emergent trait of advisor factions? Mau's decision was individual; Yapese and Marshallese secrecy was institutional.
- The Hansa's Einung (no decision if anyone objects) and the Althing's arbitrated-majority are opposite consensus rules. Which does the act-3 assembly use, or is it a culture parameter?
- Windows in the belt are deterministic. Is there a design reason to add weather-like variance (solar storms, feedstock quality) so that "reading the swell" has an analog, or should uncertainty live entirely in the hull's condition and the crew's skill?
