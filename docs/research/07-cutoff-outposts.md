# 07 — Outposts that got cut off

What happens when the ship stops coming. Historical grounding for the act 1 -> act 2 transition.

Note on method: web search budget ran out mid-task; the later cases (EIC, North Korea, Cuba, Haudegen, Roanoke, Riel) were filled from direct fetches of encyclopaedic pages rather than primary literature. Figures marked (mem.) are from memory and should be checked before becoming constants.

## 1. Findings

### 1.1 Greenland Norse (c. 986 – c. 1450): the outpost that stayed loyal until it vanished

**Scale.** Two settlements, ~500 ruin groups in the Eastern Settlement. Peak population older literature 4,000–5,000; current scholarship ~2,000–2,500. About 20% of ruin groups are single structures; 3% are large high-status farms (Arneborg et al. 2012). This is a *small* population by any state standard, and it persisted 450+ years.

**Dependence.** The economy had two layers. Subsistence was pastoral (cattle for prestige, sheep/goats for calories) plus seal hunting; the export layer was almost entirely walrus ivory, which Greenland supplied to Europe in near-monopoly from the 11th to 13th centuries (Barrett et al. 2018, Proc. R. Soc. B — 67 rostra, aDNA on 25). The hunt was at the Nordrsetur, hundreds of km north of the settlements (Disko Bay, later toward Smith Sound); Barrett notes "the brief window of summer would have barely been sufficient for rowing the many hundreds of miles north and back." Over time the tusks come from smaller, more often female animals and from further north — classic resource-curse depletion. Elephant ivory flooded Europe in the 13th century; by 1282 the Pope asked for Greenland's tithe in silver rather than tusks; there is little evidence of walrus ivory reaching mainland Europe after 1400.

**The supply chain was political before it failed physically.** In 1261 the Greenlanders submitted to the Norwegian crown *in exchange for a promised shipping connection*; in 1294 a royal monopoly restricted trade to licensed vessels — one or two ships a year through the 14th century. The Greenland knarr sank in 1369 and was apparently not replaced. Greenland has no timber; the colony could not build its own ocean-going ship. The last documented voyage left in 1410 (Icelanders who had arrived 1406). The last dated event is a wedding at Hvalsey church, 16 September 1408, recorded because the couple later returned to Iceland — the church was described as full. So: 1261 dependence formalised → 1369 loss of the vessel → ~1410 last contact → Eastern Settlement gone by ~1450. Roughly **40 years from last ship to end** for a population of ~2,000, after ~150 years of a thinning link.

**Did they adapt?** Yes, materially. Bone collagen isotopes show early settlers ate 20–30% marine protein; by the 15th century some individuals were at ~80% marine (Arneborg et al. 2012; the Radiocarbon paper). Seal bones are as common as cattle bones in late middens. Dugmore et al. (PNAS 2012) frame the end as "cultural adaptation, compounding vulnerabilities and conjunctures": the Norse *did* adapt (communal seal drives, diet shift) but each adaptation narrowed their margins, and a conjuncture — Little Ice Age cooling (0.5–1 °C below today), the ivory price collapse, isostatic sea-level rise of up to ~3.3 m drowning the best lowland pasture (PNAS 2023), and a population too small to absorb losses — ended them. Diamond's "failure to adapt" story is contested, but one piece survives: there is no good evidence they adopted Inuit kayak/toggling-harpoon technology.

**What the last generations looked like.** The Herjolfsnes graves hold garments in 15th-century continental European fashion (the "Burgundian" hat, redated to ~1400–1450). The bishop's seat at Gardar was vacant from 1378 but the church hierarchy stayed oriented to Rome and Nidaros. No mass grave, no burned farms, no evidence of famine as the sole cause; church valuables were removed, which reads as orderly departure or gradual out-migration to Iceland/Norway. The point for us: **the Greenland Norse never went Voidborn.** They stayed a European province in identity while their material life became Arctic, and when the identity could no longer be sustained they left or died rather than becoming something new. That is the *failed* branch of act 2, and it is the default branch for a small colony with a strong metropolitan church and no ability to build its own vessel.

### 1.2 Pitcairn (1790–): a massacre becomes a model village in one generation

**Founding party.** 9 British mutineers, 6 Polynesian men, 12 Tahitian women and one girl (Steptoe & Wood; Wikipedia gives 19 women kidnapped at Tahiti, 6 released at Moorea). Sex ratio 15 men to 12 women. The mutineers burned the *Bounty* five days after landing (23 January 1790) — an irreversible commitment to the outpost. Land was divided among the nine Europeans only; the Polynesians were treated as labour. Two immediate structural faults: unequal partners and unpartnered men.

**Violence.** When a mutineer's wife died, he took a Polynesian's wife. 20 September 1793: the four remaining Polynesian men killed five Europeans in coordinated attacks (including Fletcher Christian). The women then killed three of the Polynesians; the fourth was killed later. 1798: McCoy, having distilled spirits from ti root, killed himself; 1799: Quintal, drunk and threatening the whole community, was killed with a hatchet by Adams and Young. By 1800: **one adult man, nine or ten women, ~19 children.** So 3.5 years to the first killings, 10 years to the end of the founding generation of men. Alcohol and the sex ratio are the proximate mechanisms in almost every death.

**The religious turn.** Young (literate) and Adams (barely) turned to the ship's Bible and prayer book, taught the children to read from it, and built a strict pious order. Young died 1800. When the American sealer *Topaz* found the island (February 1808) and HMS *Briton*/*Tagus* in 1814, they found "46 mainly young islanders led by Adams" — polite, literate, teetotal, English-speaking with a Tahitian-inflected creole (Pitkern) already forming. Adams feared trial for mutiny; instead the Royal Navy in effect pardoned him and made him a curiosity. **Eighteen years from landing to recognisably distinct culture**, with one authority figure and one text.

**Second generation and after.** 1831: the British moved the whole population (87) to Tahiti for "overcrowding"; 12 died of disease within months and the rest went home. 1832–38: Joshua Hill, an outsider, arrived claiming (falsely) British government backing, got himself elected "President", ran a temperance tyranny with a hierarchy of Elders and Cadets, flogged and imprisoned islanders, and was removed only when a naval captain investigated. The 1838 constitution (Capt. Elliott, HMS *Fly*) that followed created an annually elected magistrate, compulsory schooling, and votes for all adults including women — the first community to enfranchise women in the modern era. 1856: all ~190 moved to Norfolk Island; 16–18 came back within two years, 27 more in 1864. Pitcairn population peaked at 233 in 1937 and was 47 by 2021. The 2004 trials convicted six men of decades of child sexual abuse, showing the dark side of a founder culture: norms that no outside authority tests for generations.

### 1.3 The VOC Cape Colony → Afrikaners (1652–1806): the company manufactures a people

This is the most important case for act 2, because the *sponsor's own policies* produced the new ethnos, and the ethnos' politics were defined by grievance against the sponsor for 140 years before it was ever cut off.

**Timeline and numbers.**
- 1652: refreshment station for VOC fleets, explicitly not a colony.
- 1657: 9 employees released as "free burghers" to farm, obliged to sell to the Company at fixed prices; 1662: 35 burghers, 15 women, 22 children, 134 officials, 180 slaves.
- 1688: ~200 Huguenots (about a sixth of the European population) — Calvinist reinforcement.
- 1701: 1,265 Europeans; 1706: immigration restricted; 1713: smallpox kills ~25% of Cape Town Dutch and ~90% of the local Khoi — clearing the interior.
- 1716: ~2,000 burghers, 744 officials, ~2,700 slaves. 1717: the Heren XVII decide to stop assisted European immigration and rely on slave labour (mem.). From here on **growth is natural increase**: 5,419 Europeans by 1753, 14,929 by 1795 (with ~17,000 slaves; 61,947 total in 1797). By 1795 the great majority of burghers had never seen Europe.
- 1706: Adam Tas and the burghers petition Amsterdam against Governor W. A. van der Stel's monopolisation of farms and contracts; Tas jailed in the Castle; the Company sides with the burghers and recalls the governor. 7 March 1707: Hendrik Biebouw, being punished, shouts "Ik ben een Africaander" — **55 years after founding**, the first self-naming.
- 1714: loan-farm (leningplaats) system — ~6,000 acres for an annual fee — makes pastoral dispersal cheap. Trekboers reach the Fish River by 1770: ~800 km of frontier in 60 years, entirely outside effective Company administration.
- 1779–85: Cape Patriots petition for burgher representation and free trade.
- 1795: with the VOC bankrupt and the Netherlands overrun, the burghers of Graaff-Reinet and then Swellendam expel the landdrost and declare republics — *months before* the British arrive. The first true cutoff produced instant frontier self-government.
- 1795/1806 British occupation; 1815 Slagtersnek rising; 1836–40 Great Trek (~12–15,000, mem.) — a second exodus from a second metropole, carrying the same grievances (prices, representation, labour).

**Mechanisms.** (1) The Company's monopsony at fixed prices was the constant grievance; every generation's politics is about it. (2) The Company deliberately stopped immigration, so the population became locally born; the sponsor's cost-cutting created the demographic basis of a new people. (3) The mobile pastoral offshoot (trekboers) escaped both price control and administration — the Belt analogue is a prospector/hauler fleet living beyond the sponsor's docking radius. (4) Language: Afrikaans arose as a koine/creole among slaves, Khoi and burghers ("kitchen Dutch"), first written in Arabic script by Cape Muslims; the metropole regarded it as debased for another century. (5) Religion: a Calvinist chosen-people frame gave the grievance a theology. (6) Identity was defined *against* the Company before it was defined against anyone else.

### 1.4 Micro-societies: Tristan da Cunha, St Kilda, Palmerston

**Tristan da Cunha.** British garrison 1816; withdrawn November 1817; Corporal William Glass, wife, two children and two stonemasons stayed. Their founding document (1817) declared all stock and stores shared equally and "no member shall assume any superiority whatever." 1824: 22 men, 3 women — the sex ratio was solved by importing five women from St Helena in 1827. 1856: 97; 1857: 45 left with the departing chaplain (the metropole as migration sink); 1873: 86 in 15 families. 1885: 15 men drowned trying to reach a passing ship, leaving "an island of widows". 1892: two Italian sailors add carpentry skills. **No ship called at all between 1909 and 1919**; they learned the First World War was over in 1919. WWII brought a naval station, school, hospital and a cash store. 1961: volcanic eruption, all 264 evacuated to England; in 1963 the great majority chose to return, against official expectations. Founder genetics: ~15 effective founders, 7 surnames; 57% of islanders have some asthma; retinitis pigmentosa elevated. Governance today: Island Council with an elected Chief Islander, land held communally, equal livestock quotas per household (a direct descendant of the 1817 compact). Population 221–250.

**St Kilda.** ~180 (1697); 1727 smallpox left too few to man the boats and the island was restocked from Harris; 88 (1758); 112 (1851). The "parliament": every morning all adult men met in the street, no chairman, everyone spoke, the day's work was decided; contentious but never feuded permanently. 1852: 36 emigrated to Australia (18 died en route), leaving 76. Neonatal tetanus (from unclean cord-dressing): of 56 live births 1855–76 only 15 survived — infant mortality up to 80%. From the 1870s steamers brought tourists and a cash economy, and disease; the islanders became dependent on imports. WWI: a naval detachment gave daily communication and regular supply for the first time; when it was withdrawn in 1919 the young left, "resulting in a breakdown of the island economy". 73 (1920) → 37 (1928) → 36 evacuated at their own petition, 29 August 1930. Lessons: (a) a period of *good* connection followed by withdrawal is worse than steady isolation; (b) below ~40 people with a skewed age structure the community cannot crew its own boats and is finished; (c) egalitarian consensus government works at this scale.

**Palmerston.** 1863: one Englishman with three Cook-Islander wives (a chief's daughter and her two cousins); 23 children, 134 grandchildren. He divided the island and its motu three ways by wife; today a council of the three family heads plus one appointee each, mayor rotating among the heads; marriage within a lineage forbidden. Population ~62, all but three descended from him; 1860s Gloucestershire English preserved. Viable only because Rarotonga acts as a reservoir and sink.

### 1.5 Trade-post frontiers: Russian America and the Métis

**Russian-American Company / Fort Ross.** Chartered 1799; Russian America was never self-sufficient in food; supply from Okhotsk was irregular and often spoiled. Fort Ross (1812) was founded explicitly as a farm for Alaska and never met expectations even at its early-1830s peak; the sea otter was hunted out; sold to Sutter in 1841 for $30,000 he never paid. From 1839 the RAC bought its food from the Hudson's Bay Company. Almost no Russian women came; the 1821 charter created a legal estate of "Creoles" — children of Russian fathers and Native mothers — free of tax but bound to Company service after education. Creoles went from ~200 (1818) to ~2,000 (1863) and outnumbered ethnic Russians (~600–800) at most posts, filling navigation, clerical and skilled roles. Aleuts owed compulsory hunting labour. A new people arose *inside the Company's own personnel classification*; the sale of Alaska (1867) orphaned them.

**Hudson's Bay posts and the Métis.** Country marriages between traders and Cree/Ojibwe women produced a mixed population from the 1750s in the Great Lakes; they became provisioners (pemmican, buffalo hunt) and free traders. The HBC's attempt to control that economy created the nation: the 1814 Pemmican Proclamation banned export of pemmican from Red River; the Métis arrested the governor; 19 June 1816 at Seven Oaks Cuthbert Grant's party killed the HBC governor Semple and ~20 men. By then they called themselves *la nouvelle nation* and flew their own flag. 1849 Sayer trial: an armed Métis crowd forced the HBC court to release a free trader — "le commerce est libre" — ending the monopoly in practice. 1869–70: the HBC sold Rupert's Land to Canada for £300,000 without consulting anyone living there; Red River then held ~12,000 people, about 5,800 French-speaking Métis, 4,100 English-speaking Métis, ~1,600 whites (1870 census, mem.). Riel's provisional government claimed legitimacy on the precise argument that **HBC authority had lapsed and Canada's had not yet begun**, so by the law of nations the inhabitants could form a government; it issued a List of Rights and negotiated the Manitoba Act (1870: province status, bilingual institutions, 1.4 million acres for Métis children). The promises were then defeated by scrip fraud and an incoming Ontario majority; most Métis moved west; 1885 the second provisional government was crushed at Batoche and Riel hanged. Timeline: ~60 years from post-frontier to "New Nation"; contact/absorption via negotiation, then betrayal, then war, in 15 years.

### 1.6 Failed and near-failed starts: Roanoke, Jamestown, Plymouth

- **Roanoke 1587:** ~115 colonists including women and children, no soldiers. Governor White sailed for supplies in August 1587; the Armada emergency (and a Counter-Armada) blocked every ship until 1590. He found the site fortified but empty, "CROATOAN" carved on a post — the pre-agreed signal. Tree rings show 1587–89 as the worst drought in 800 years. Best hypothesis: absorption into the Croatan (Lawson in 1701 met Hatteras people claiming white ancestors). **Three years without a ship and the colony simply ceased to be a colony** — it merged with the neighbours.
- **Jamestown:** 104 men landed May 1607; 38 alive by January 1608. 1609–10 "starving time": ~500 down to ~61 after the relief fleet's flagship wrecked with the provisions; dogs, cats, rats, leather, then cannibalism (the 14-year-old "Jane", butchery marks confirmed 2013). In June 1610 the survivors abandoned the fort and were sailing out when Lord De La Warr's ships met them in the river. The sponsor kept shipping bodies: ~6,000 sent by 1624, ~1,200 alive (mem.).
- **Plymouth:** 102 landed; ~45–50 died the first winter; survival depended on Wampanoag diplomacy and Squanto's agronomy. Financially the colony was owned by the Merchant Adventurers; in 1627 twelve "Undertakers" (8 colonists, 4 Londoners) assumed the £1,800 debt in exchange for the fur monopoly; debt finally cleared 1641–48. **Twenty-plus years to buy out the sponsor.**

### 1.7 Company becomes state: EIC and the BSAC/Rhodesia

**EIC.** Plassey 1757; 1765 the Diwani (revenue rights over Bengal, Bihar, Orissa). Bullion exports from London to India fell from ~£140,000/yr (1760–65) to ~£18,000/yr (1772–75): **the outpost stopped needing the metropole's capital because it taxed locally**. 1769–73 Bengal famine, 7–10 million dead under Company tax-farming; 1772 the Company needed a state loan to avoid bankruptcy; 1773 Regulating Act, 1784 Board of Control, 1813/1833 trade monopoly abolished — the Company became a pure administration; army 70,000 (1796) → 154,500 (1806) → 350,000 (1857); 1858 nationalised after the Rebellion. The pattern: local agents acquire sovereignty from local revenue; the metropole answers with regulation, then nationalisation.

**BSAC / Rhodesia.** Royal charter October 1889 for 25 years; occupation 1890; wars 1893 and 1896–97; charter extended 10 years in 1915. Settler grievances: income tax flowing to shareholders, no control of budgets, and in 1918 the Privy Council ruled that unalienated land belonged to the Crown, not the Company — the Company's whole asset base evaporated. 1922 referendum among ~34,000 Europeans: self-government beat joining South Africa (about 8,800 to 6,000, mem.); charter revoked, responsible government 1 October 1923. Then 1965 UDI: the proclamation declared loyalty to the Queen personally while rebelling against her government; Britain called it treason; the settlers dropped the monarchy only in 1970. Two-step: settlers evict the company, then evict the metropole, each time claiming to be the *more* loyal party.

### 1.8 Modern cases

**Soviet polar network at the collapse.** The last Soviet drifting station NP-31 was closed in July 1991; the programme stopped for 12 years (resumed 2003). Vostok — 15 winterers, resupplied by tractor traverse from Mirny ~1,400 km away — closed from February to November 1994 for lack of money and transport; in April 1982 a fire had destroyed its generators and 20 men survived the winter on improvised diesel stoves (one died, mem.). Pyramiden on Svalbard cut its last coal 31 March 1998 and was abandoned in place; Barentsburg continued. What this shows: modern stations don't drift, they *close*; a station of 15–30 rotating specialists has no children and no economy and is a garrison, not a community. The collapse of the sponsor produced abandonment within 1–7 years, never a new society.

**State-scale cutoffs (the closest modern analogue to "the ships stop").** North Korea after 1991: energy imports fell 75%; rice harvest 3.56 Mt (1993) → 1.40 Mt (1995); the state ration fell from 470 g/day (1994) to 128 g (1997) and by 1997 fed 6% of the population; deaths 240,000–3.5 million (best estimates 500–600k); ~250,000 crossed into China; the illegal *jangmadang* markets became the real economy and have never been suppressed; the regime answered with "military first". Cuba after 1991: GDP −35%, imports and exports −80%, oil at 10% of pre-1990; calories 3,052/day (1989) → 2,099 (1993), 1,450 for the elderly and children; an optic-neuropathy epidemic; oxen replaced tractors, 1.2 million bicycles imported, urban organic agriculture; dollar legalised 1993; ~5 years to regain GDP. The regime that loosened (Cuba) had hardship; the one that held the plan rigid (North Korea) had famine — but in both, **a bottom-up grey economy emerged within 2–3 years and became permanent**.

**Garrisons that outlived their orders.** Onoda (Lubang, 1944–74): ordered to hold and never surrender, told "we'll come back for you"; treated every leaflet, newspaper and search party as a trick ("they would not have fired on us before if the war had ended"); his cell killed up to 30 Filipinos; one companion surrendered 1950, one killed 1954, one 1972; he stopped only when his former commanding officer flew out and rescinded the order in person. Baler (1898–99): 50–57 Spanish soldiers held a church for 337 days, refused Spanish newspapers as forgeries; 19 died (15 of disease); surrendered when a detail in a newspaper convinced them. Haudegen (Svalbard): ~11 German meteorologists told of the surrender by radio on 8 May 1945, then lost contact, could not raise anyone until August, were picked up by a Norwegian sealer on 4 September 1945. **Clipperton (1914–17):** ~100 Mexicans supplied every two months from Acapulco; the Revolution ended the ships; scurvy killed the men; the lighthouse keeper Álvarez collected every firearm, threw all but one in the sea and declared himself king, raping and murdering for two years until the women killed him with a hammer in July 1917 — the same day a US warship arrived to take off 11 survivors (3 women, 8 children).

**Shipwreck micro-cases (weeks-to-months timescale).** *Batavia* 1629: ~282 survivors on waterless islets; the commander left for help (33 days to Java); the senior remaining VOC official, Cornelisz, was accepted as leader by rank, took control of weapons and food, sent the 20-odd soldiers to a distant islet on a pretext, bound followers by oath and by making them kill, and murdered 110–125 people in about six weeks — until the soldiers, who had found water, built a coral fort and beat off attacks; the rescue ship arrived ~17 September. Auckland Islands 1864: the *Grafton*'s five men wrote rules on a page of the Bible and read them every Sunday, elected the captain as revocable chief, rotated cooking, ran an evening school, built a forge, made hundreds of nails and a decked boat, and all five survived 19 months; the *Invercauld*'s 19 survivors kept shipboard rank (officers had boys fetch limpets), the captain gave no orders, the sick were abandoned on beaches, one man ate another, and three survived 12 months.

### 1.9 Cross-cutting answers

**Timeline from "supplies stop" to "we are a different people".**
- Weeks: power struggle over food and weapons (Batavia ~6 weeks; Invercauld days).
- 1–4 years: founding trauma (Pitcairn killings at 3.5 y; Clipperton 2 y; Seven Oaks 2 y after the Pemmican Proclamation).
- ~15–20 years: a distinct local culture visible to outsiders (Pitcairn 1808; Adams' Bible school).
- ~50–60 years: self-naming and a political programme (Afrikaner 1707 at 55 y; Métis "New Nation" ~60 y from ethnogenesis; Red River 1816).
- 100–150 years: armed republics / demand for self-rule (Graaff-Reinet 1795 at 143 y; Riel 1870).
- The Greenland counter-case: 450 years without ever becoming a new people, and ~40 years from last ship to extinction.

**Accelerants:** children born in place becoming the majority (Cape after 1717; RAC creoles by 1860; Pitcairn by 1814); a founding trauma with an us/them story; a text or creed (Bible, compact, Calvinism, List of Rights); a new language (Pitkern, Afrikaans, Michif); intermarriage with locals (Métis, creoles, Pitcairn, Roanoke's absorption); an economy the sponsor cannot reach (trekboers, buffalo hunt, jangmadang); grievance defined against the sponsor's price/labour rules.

**Retardants:** rotating personnel with careers at home (RAC managers, Antarctic winterers); a prestige hierarchy oriented to the metropole (Gardar bishopric, Herjolfsnes fashion); no local reproduction (St Kilda, all garrisons); standing orders and the expectation of relief (Onoda, Baler); regular arrivals of new settlers who reset the culture (Jamestown's 6,000).

**Who takes power, and with what legitimacy.**
1. Senior surviving rank (Cornelisz, Dalgarno): legitimacy of the org chart. Catastrophic when rank ≠ competence.
2. The last literate man with the text (Adams): scriptural legitimacy; produced stability plus a closed morality.
3. Compact and election (Glass 1817, Musgrave 1864, St Kilda parliament): consent; works at 5–200 people.
4. The impostor with a forged metropolitan mandate (Joshua Hill 1832–38): works for years in a small community starved of outside information.
5. "Their authority has lapsed; ours begins" (Riel 1869, Graaff-Reinet 1795, Rhodesia 1923/1965): the interregnum argument — the strongest claim for act 2, and one the metropole never accepts.
6. The last order (Onoda, Baler, Haudegen): only the issuer can revoke it; produces violence against neighbours.
7. Whoever holds the guns (Álvarez): ends with the guns being taken.

**When contact resumes.** Pardon and curiosity (Pitcairn 1808–14; Haudegen; Onoda). Emigration sink — the metropole drains the outpost (Pitcairn 1831, 1856; Tristan 1857; St Kilda 1852, 1930). Negotiation-then-betrayal-then-war (Manitoba 1870 → 1885). Annexation → rebellion → exodus → war (Cape 1795 → 1815 → 1836 → 1880/1899). Settlers evict company then metropole, claiming superior loyalty (Rhodesia). Nobody there (Greenland 1721, Roanoke 1590). Sponsor nationalised by its own state (EIC 1858). Note that resumed contact is *never neutral*: it either drains people, or imposes a governor, or both.

**Demographics.** Founding sizes that persisted: Pitcairn 27 → 46 in 24 years → 190 by 1856; Tristan ~15 effective founders → 97 in 40 years; Palmerston 4 → 62 resident plus a diaspora; RAC creoles 200 → 2,000 in 45 years; Cape 35 burghers/15 women (1662) → ~15,000 by 1795 with total European immigration of only a few thousand (implies ~2–2.5%/yr natural growth). Sex ratio: every case with unpartnered men above ~1.3:1 had lethal violence or had to import women (Pitcairn 15:12 → massacre; Tristan 22:3 → five women from St Helena; Jamestown 0 women; RAC → a creole class). Genetics: Smith (2014) gives 14,000–44,000 for a *closed* 150-year population to stay healthy; Marin & Beluffi (2018) find 98 sufficient with strictly managed pairing; Salotti (2020) 110 for a Mars base on labour grounds. Empirically, 15–30 founders persisted 200 years with **occasional** inflow (Tristan gained ~10 founders over the 19th century) at the price of a founder disease load (57% asthma). Take: social viability, not genetic, sets the floor — ~40 is death (St Kilda), ~60–100 survives with a reservoir, ~250 is stable, and the genetic cost of a small founder pool is a slow penalty, not a cliff, as long as a few outsiders marry in per generation.

### 1.10 A pattern language for the cutoff

Each stage: the historical trigger, the fork, and where it points in a game. Mechanics are elaborated in §3.

### 1.11 The ways it goes badly (all attested; a game should allow each)

1. **Cult massacre by the senior surviving official** (Batavia): rank-based leader + oath + control of stores + separated soldiers → 40% killed in six weeks. Antidote historically: an armed minority with its own water.
2. **Tyrant of the last gun** (Clipperton): once the men die, whoever kept a firearm rules by rape and murder; ended by the victims.
3. **Rank paralysis** (Invercauld): officers keep privileges, no one organises, the weak are abandoned, cannibalism; 3 of 19 live.
4. **Sex-ratio war** (Pitcairn): unpartnered men plus alcohol → 13 of 15 men dead in ten years.
5. **Pious closed order with hidden abuse** (Pitcairn 1800–2004): stability bought with a morality nobody outside can test.
6. **Impostor capture** (Joshua Hill): a small, information-starved community hands power to anyone with a plausible mandate.
7. **Holdout violence** (Onoda): a garrison keeps executing its last order against neighbours for decades.
8. **Denial** (Baler, Greenland fashion): refusing to believe the metropole is gone; over-investing in the return.
9. **Starvation spiral** (Jamestown, North Korea): rations fall below ~130 g/day; the state feeds only itself; deaths in the tens of percent; escape by the able-bodied.
10. **Emigration sink** (St Kilda, Pitcairn, Tristan 1857): every renewed contact drains the young; below ~40 with no children the outpost cannot crew its own boats.
11. **Loyal extinction** (Greenland): adapt materially, stay metropolitan in identity, then leave or die when the identity cannot be fed.
12. **Absorption by neighbours** (Roanoke): the outpost dissolves into the locals — not a bad outcome for the people, but the end of the polity.
13. **Frontier secession** (Graaff-Reinet, trekboers): the mobile faction splits the polity the moment the centre weakens.
14. **Betrayed negotiation** (Manitoba 1870 → 1885): terms agreed with the returning power, then hollowed out; war 15 years later.

## 3. Implications for mechanics

Each item: mechanic — evidence — act.

1. **Hull ownership as the master variable of dependence.** Track whether the outpost *owns and can maintain/replace* interplanetary hulls, or only leases sponsor hulls with rotating crews. Losing the last owned hull with no replacement capability is the Greenland-1369 event: it starts a countdown to the "silent year." — Greenland (no timber → no ship), Clipperton (no boat), Grafton (built one). — Act 1 seed; act 2 trigger. Matches NOTES' "fleet = delta-v = freedom".

2. **Single-export resource curse.** The sponsor prices one commodity; over time yield per sortie falls (further hunting grounds, smaller animals) while the price falls too. The sim should model *distance-to-resource creep* and a sponsor-side price that can collapse for reasons invisible to the player (elephant ivory). — Barrett 2018/2020. — Act 1.

3. **The sponsor's labour policy manufactures the people.** Give the sponsor levers that are individually rational and collectively ethnogenic: release contract workers as "free" settlers with sale-at-fixed-price obligations; stop sending new personnel; rely on locally born labour. Each raises the locally-born share and the grievance index. — Cape 1657/1717, RAC creole estate 1821. — Act 1 (sponsor AI behaviour), pays off in act 2.

4. **Birthplace share as the culture clock.** NOTES already has birthplace = culture seed. Calibrate: distinct culture visible when locally born ≥ ~50% of adults (Pitcairn ~1808), self-naming at ~2 generations (Cape 55 y), political programme at ~3 (Métis, Cape republics). — §1.9. — Act 2.

5. **The "silent year" and information trust.** When the expected ship fails, the outpost does not know if the sponsor is late, bankrupt, or gone. Model a belief state: leaflets/relays can be "enemy tricks"; advisors split between "they're coming" and "they're gone". Over-investment in the return (Herjolfsnes hats, Baler) should be a real, tempting, wrong choice. — Onoda, Baler, Greenland. — Act 1→2 transition.

6. **Weapons locker and stores custody as the first act-2 decision.** Within the first turns after the cutoff the player must decide who holds arms and rations: the senior sponsor officer (rank), an elected council (compact), or nobody (dispersal). Rank + oath + separated soldiers = Batavia path; shared chest + written rules = Grafton path. — Batavia, Clipperton, Grafton. — Act 2 opening.

7. **Sex-ratio and pair-bond pressure.** Track unpartnered adults by sex. Ratio above ~1.3 with alcohol/drug availability raises a "Pitcairn" event chain (jealousy killing → faction massacre). Relief valves: import partners (St Helena 1827), intermarry with other groups (Métis), or enforce exogamy rules (Palmerston). — §1.9. — Act 1 crew-selection seed; act 2 events.

8. **Founding trauma as identity fork.** The first killing/expulsion after the cutoff forces a choice of story: against the sponsor, against the neighbours, against a faction. The chosen story sets the direction of S8 self-naming and which act-3 alliances are possible. — Pitcairn, Seven Oaks, Adam Tas. — Act 2.

9. **The Text.** A written compact, creed or list of rights, once adopted, is a persistent object with a re-reading ritual that reduces internal violence but raises rigidity and hides abuse. Variants: egalitarian compact (Tristan; equal shares, no superiority), scriptural school (Adams; stability + closed morality), list of rights (Riel; negotiating position for act 3). — §1.9. — Act 2.

10. **The offshoot faction.** A mobile sub-group with its own delta-v drifts beyond the settlement's reach (trekboers, buffalo hunt, prospectors). It gains resources, loses culture-sync, and returns as a separate polity with the same grievance. Let it go (lose control), chase (lose fleet), or follow (become nomads). — Cape, Métis. — Act 2, seeds act 3's "other groups".

11. **Grey market emergence under rationing.** When official distribution falls below a threshold (NK: 128 g/day; feeding 6%), an informal economy appears within 2–3 years and becomes permanent. The player can suppress (famine), tolerate (Cuba, faster recovery), or legalise. — NK, Cuba. — Act 2.

12. **The impostor event.** Years into isolation, an arrival claims a mandate from the sponsor/metropole. Information latency decides whether it can be checked. Accepting a false mandate gives short-term order and long-term tyranny; refusing a true one has its own costs. — Joshua Hill. — Act 2 / early act 3.

13. **The interregnum claim.** When contact resumes, the outpost's strongest legitimacy argument is "your authority lapsed; ours began" — a provisional government with a list of rights. Model the returning power's response as a menu: pardon-and-absorb, negotiate-then-hollow-out, install a governor, or purchase. Terms not backed by physical leverage (volatiles, fleet) get hollowed out within ~15 years (Manitoba). — Riel, Graaff-Reinet, Rhodesia. — Act 3, but the leverage is built in act 2.

14. **Resumed contact as emigration sink.** Every ship that returns drains young people unless the outpost has something to keep them; below ~40 with no births the outpost cannot crew its own vessels. Model an "attractiveness" differential and let the metropole win it by default. — St Kilda 1852/1930, Pitcairn 1831/1856, Tristan 1857. — Act 3 threat; also an act-2 risk if any neighbour is richer.

15. **Rotation vs residence.** Personnel on rotation (Antarctic model) never form a society; when the sponsor dies, the station closes in 1–7 years. If the act 1 outpost is staffed by rotators, act 2 is abandonment, not ethnogenesis. So act 1 must let the player (or the sponsor's cost-cutting) convert rotators into residents. — Vostok, NP-31, Pyramiden vs Cape. — Act 1.

16. **Capability decay.** Skills not exercised locally decay per generation; the Greenland Norse lost the ability to reach Europe, Pitcairn lost navigation, Tristan gained carpentry only when two Italians washed up. Tie NOTES' "capability" axis to a per-generation decay unless taught, and let random arrivals (wrecks, refugees) inject skills. — §1.4, 1.1. — Act 2.

17. **Loyal extinction as a legitimate ending.** The colony that adapts materially but keeps metropolitan identity and church, and departs or dies when that cannot be fed, is a real historical outcome and should be a reachable, dignified "loss" — not a game over screen but a diaspora epilogue. — Greenland. — Act 2.

18. **Holdout mode.** A faction that treats the sponsor's last orders as binding can persist for decades and harm neighbours; only a credible revocation by the issuing authority ends it. Useful for a sponsor-loyalist faction in act 2 that becomes an act-3 problem when the inner system returns. — Onoda, Baler. — Act 2–3.

19. **Absorption into neighbours.** If a friendly, larger, better-adapted group exists (Croatan, Inuit), the outpost may dissolve into it — a peaceful loss of the polity. Requires "other groups" from NOTES to exist in act 2 as cultural attractors, not just rivals. — Roanoke. — Act 2.

20. **Egalitarian micro-governance works at small scale.** The daily all-hands parliament (St Kilda), council of lineage heads with rotating mayor (Palmerston), Island Council with communal land and equal livestock quotas (Tristan) are all stable for 100–300 years at 40–250 people. Above a few hundred, the Cape and Red River needed representative institutions and produced the grievance about not having them. The sim's governance options should scale with population. — §1.4. — Act 2.

## 4. Open questions

- **Which act-1 sponsor behaviour is the default?** Chartered-company (Cape/HBC) behaviour — fixed prices, labour release, immigration stop — produces ethnogenesis; agency/station behaviour (Antarctic rotation) produces abandonment. The design must decide which the sponsor AI does, or let it drift from one to the other as costs bite. Interacts with 04-economics.
- **How much of the "silent year" belief state to expose to the player.** Realism says the player should not know whether the sponsor is late or dead; playability says advisors need to argue about it. Design decision.
- **Genetics.** Do we simulate founder-effect disease load (a slow penalty), or abstract it into a "founder pool size" number? The literature disagrees by two orders of magnitude on MVP (98 vs 14,000+); the historical evidence says a few hundred with occasional inflow is fine. Suggest the latter, with a per-generation penalty if no inflow.
- **Intermarriage with "locals".** Every ethnogenesis case involved a local population to marry into. The belt has none. The functional substitutes are refugees, wreck survivors, other outposts and the offshoot faction — 06-sea-peoples and act 2's "other groups" need to supply them, or act 2 is Pitcairn-without-Tahitians (i.e., Tristan: slow, inbred, egalitarian).
- **Violence budget.** How far the game lets the Batavia/Clipperton branches run. The historical rate is high (40% dead in six weeks); a sim that only allows polite drift misrepresents the evidence. Needs a content decision.
- **Resumption terms.** Whether act 3 negotiation uses an explicit "list of rights" object seeded in act 2, and how the returning power's hollowing-out (Manitoba) is modelled.
- Unverified figures flagged (mem.) above: 1717 immigration decision, Red River 1870 census breakdown, 1922 referendum tally, Vostok 1982 fire, Tristan 1885 adult-male count, Jamestown 1624 totals.
