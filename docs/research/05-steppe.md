# 05 — Steppe nomads: the settled-society assumptions they broke

Organised around inverted assumptions, not around the steppe. Each subsection
states the sedentary assumption, what the steppe did instead, and the mechanism
that made it work. Section 3 restates the inversions as a list, each paired with
a candidate mechanic, an act, and where the belt analogy breaks.

## 1. Findings

### 1.0 Framing: the two scholarly fights that matter for the sim

Two live disagreements decide what a "nomad model" even is. The sim should treat
both as tunable parameters rather than pick a side.

**Fight 1 — needy nomad vs affluent nomad.** Khazanov (1984) established the
baseline: pastoral nomadism is a specialised economy that is *never autarkic*.
Grain, metal, and textiles must come from outside; the nomad's political
options are therefore shaped by the neighbouring farmers. Barfield (1989) built
on this: steppe empires are "shadow empires," secondary formations that rise and
fall in step with a unified China because their revenue is extracted from it.
Di Cosmo (2002) and Beckwith (2009) reject the "needy nomad." Di Cosmo shows the
Xiongnu grew cereals, ran their own Central Asian trade networks, and formed a
state in response to a *military crisis* (Qin expansion into the Ordos, 215 BC),
not an appetite for grain; he argues the walls were as much offensive as
defensive. Beckwith goes further: rank-and-file nomads were better fed, better
clothed, and freer than peasants; when nomads raided they took livestock and
people, not grain, so trade rather than survival drove the economy; and the
Silk Road was an entire economy the steppe sat inside, not a pipeline it
parasitised. Kradin sits between: nomad empires are "xenocratic" — they
exercise power over outsiders and are structured by that outward-facing
extraction.

For the sim this is the question "how dependent is the belt on the inner
system, and does the dependence weaken with capability?" Barfield's answer is
"always dependent; the politics is the management of dependence." Beckwith's is
"dependent on *exchange*, not on charity, and the exchange runs both ways."
Both agree on the mechanism that matters most: **the leader's power is his
ability to bring in external goods and redistribute them.**

**Fight 2 — tribe as kinship vs tribe as politics.** The classical picture
(segmentary lineages, clans, tribes) was already under attack by Lindner (1982,
"What was a nomadic tribe?"), Golden, and Fletcher: a steppe "tribe" is a
political following that acquires a genealogy afterwards. Sneath (2007, *The
Headless State*) pushes to the limit: the clan/tribe picture is a colonial and
ethnographic artefact; Inner Asian society was organised by *hereditary
aristocratic orders* — nobles (noyan, taiji) with assigned commoner households
(albatu) grouped into administrative units (otog, khoshuu) that were reassigned
by decree, sometimes across hundreds of kilometres. Kinship was the idiom, not
the structure; the "state" was there all along, just headless — distributed
among the aristocracy rather than concentrated in a court. Reviewers (e.g. the
*Social Evolution & History* review essay, 2010) find him ambitious but
overreaching: he neglects Russian-language ethnography, real clan networks among
Buryats, and offers no alternative model. Golden's middle position is the safest
to simulate: **tribes are political constructs with a ruling clan at the core,
membership is fluid, and descent ideology is retrofitted onto political
success.**

### 1.1 Property and wealth

**Sedentary assumption:** wealth is land and buildings; it accumulates, is
stored, and is inherited as a fixed estate. Rich = owns more immovable capital.

**Steppe practice:**

- *Livestock is private, pasture is political.* Households own animals
  absolutely; grazing land is held by the group and allocated by its leader
  (Khazanov's dichotomy). You cannot own the steppe, only the right to be at a
  given place at a given season.
- *Wealth is volatile, not cumulative.* Herds grow fast (sheep flocks can add
  15–25 %/yr in good years) and crash faster. The 2009–10 Mongolian zud killed
  ~10.3 million animals, ~22–23 % of the national herd, in one winter; the
  1999–2002 sequence killed about 11 million over three winters. The Kazakh
  herd fell by over 80 % during 1929–33 collectivisation. No amount of skill
  makes a herd a safe store of value.
- *So wealth is converted into people.* The rich lend milking stock to poorer
  kin (Kazakh *saun*), give animals at marriages and funerals, host feasts, and
  fund followers. This buys obligation, which survives a zud when the herd does
  not. Post-disaster restocking (*zhylu*) is a kin-group obligation, i.e.
  insurance paid in prior generosity. Lattimore's line — "the pure nomad is the
  poor nomad" — is precise: a household with only herds and no network is one
  bad winter from destitution.
- *Portable prestige goods are the second store.* Silk, gold, silver, weapons,
  and captives. This is why the Han "gifts" that sound trivial to a farmer
  state (see 1.5) were the load-bearing structure of the Xiongnu state: they
  were the one form of wealth a chanyu could bank and dole out.
- *Inheritance is pre-mortem and fissive.* Sons receive herds at marriage and
  set up their own camp; the youngest son (*otchigin*, "prince of the hearth")
  stays and inherits what is left. Chinggis Khan's youngest, Tolui, kept the
  Mongol homeland; the elder sons got *uluses* on the periphery. Growth of a
  family is therefore growth in the number of camps, not in the size of one
  estate.
- *Thresholds.* In contemporary Mongolia a household with < 200 head is
  counted poor; 100–300 head is subsistence; ~1,000 is the floor of
  commercial herding; ~85 % of herding households are under 200. Herds beyond
  a few hundred cannot be worked with household labour and require hired or
  dependent herders — so wealth beyond the threshold *forces* patron–client
  relations.

**Mechanism:** wealth cannot be defended or stored, so it is spent on
obligations, which are the only asset that survives catastrophe.

### 1.2 Territory

**Sedentary assumption:** a polity is a bounded area; borders are defended;
what is inside is "ours."

**Steppe practice:**

- *The unit of value is the seasonal node and the route between nodes, not the
  area.* Kazakh camps rotate *kystau* (winter), *kokteu* (spring), *zhailau*
  (summer), *kuzdeu* (autumn). The winter camp — sheltered, with water and
  standing grass — is the scarce asset and the one that is quasi-owned and
  defended; summer pasture is abundant and used opportunistically. In the Gobi
  the well, not the pasture, is the territory.
- *Distances.* Mongolian moves are typically 10–50 km in mountain-steppe and
  100–300 km in the Gobi. Kazakh moves ran 3–360 km (Zhetysu) to 500 km
  (Yrgyz → Qostanay) and 800–1,200 km one way for the western hordes. A polity
  that must hold a 1,000-km route cannot hold a line; it holds the wells and
  crossings.
- *Sovereignty is over people, not land.* Mongol *ulus* = "the people
  given as a patrimony"; *nutag* = the pasture they have the right to use.
  Chinggis distributed *uluses* of thousands of households to sons and
  brothers, each with grazing rights attached — the map was a consequence of
  the population lists, not the other way round. Turkic *el/il* likewise means
  a people organised under a ruler. Even at the height of empire the court
  (*ordo*, whence "horde") moved seasonally; Karakorum (built 1235) was a
  depot and a stage, not where the khan lived.
- *What is defended:* winter camps, wells, the herd on the move, and the
  route's chokepoints. *What is not:* the perimeter. Attackers who arrive at
  a summer pasture find nothing.

**Mechanism:** because the productive asset renews itself (grass) and the
capital walks, the cost of ceding an area is low and the cost of losing a
node in the wrong season is total; defence follows the value.

### 1.3 Political legitimacy and organisation

**Sedentary assumption:** authority resides in an office and an apparatus;
succession is rule-governed; states expand by adding territory and taxing it.

**Steppe practice:**

- *Legitimacy is heaven-granted fortune proved by success.* Turkic *qut*,
  Mongol *su jali*, "the power of Eternal Heaven" on the Mongol seal.
  Fortune is evidenced by victory and by the flow of goods; a leader who stops
  delivering has visibly lost the mandate and his following melts away.
  Bilge Kaghan's inscription (c. 735) is a political sermon on exactly this:
  the Türks who went to the Chinese were ruined; stay with the ruling clan and
  the steppe, and heaven's favour holds.
- *Succession is "bloody tanistry" (Fletcher).* Any able adult male of the
  ruling lineage is eligible; the *quriltai* ratifies the one who has already
  assembled the winning coalition. Every death of a khan is a succession war
  or a near one: Mongol interregna 1227–29, 1241–46, 1248–51, 1259–64; the
  Xiongnu civil war of 60–53 BC that produced five rival chanyus and ended
  with Huhanye's submission to the Han. This is not dysfunction; it is the
  selection mechanism for a polity that can only be held by a demonstrably
  competent leader.
- *Barfield's imperial confederacy.* Autocratic outward (the khan monopolises
  war, diplomacy, and the extraction of goods from the sedentary world),
  consultative and federal inward (tribal leaders keep internal autonomy and
  are paid in redistributed goods). Three tiers: khan and court; imperial
  governors appointed over regions (the Xiongnu Left and Right Wise Kings, the
  twenty-four "leaders of ten thousand"); indigenous tribal chiefs. The
  glue is the flow of external goods, so the confederacy stands while the
  sedentary neighbour is rich and unified and collapses when it fragments and
  cannot pay. Barfield's cycle: Xiongnu/Han, Türk/Sui–Tang, Uyghur/Tang,
  Zunghar/Qing; the Mongols are his acknowledged anomaly.
- *Fast to form, fast to dissolve.* Temüjin's coalition took ~25 years
  (c. 1180–1206); the unified empire lasted 54 years (1206–60) before splitting
  into four khanates. The First Türk Khaganate (552) split east–west within
  ~30 years and the eastern half fell to the Tang in 630. The Uyghur state
  (744–840) fell to a Kyrgyz raid after a decade of zud and epidemic. Formation
  is a coalition of households that are *already* mobilised and armed, so it
  needs no build-up; dissolution is the same households riding away.
- *Chinggis's innovations were anti-tribal.* The decimal system (95
  *minggan* of a thousand households in 1206) deliberately mixed lineages
  under appointed commanders; the *keshig* (guard of 10,000) held the sons of
  every noble as hostage-officers; the *nökör* (free companions who chose their
  lord) were promoted over born chiefs. He was solving the confederation's
  known failure mode by converting a coalition into an administration — which
  is why the Mongols outlived the Barfield cycle for two generations.
- *Sneath's correction.* Beneath the charisma sat a hereditary aristocracy
  that survived every khan's death; commoners were assigned to nobles, not
  free members of clans. The "headless state" is what remains when the khan
  goes: an order of nobles, each with their people, still functioning.

**Mechanism:** with no fixed tax base, a leader can only pay followers from
what he extracts outside; his authority is therefore continuously re-earned
and his polity is a *coalition sized to the revenue*.

### 1.4 Kinship, adoption, and the fluidity of membership

**Sedentary assumption:** you are born into your group; kinship is a fact and
determines obligation; outsiders remain outsiders.

**Steppe practice:**

- *Kinship is made, not found.* *Anda* (sworn brotherhood: Temüjin–Jamukha,
  Yesügei–Toghril), the Scythian blood-and-wine oath (Herodotus 4.70),
  adoption (Hö'elün's four adopted "foundling" sons, one of whom, Shigi
  Qutuqu, became chief judge of the empire), *nökör* companionship. Each of
  these creates kin-grade obligation between unrelated people, by consent.
- *Marriage is alliance infrastructure.* Exogamy plus *quda* (in-law)
  partnerships: the Onggirat supplied Chinggisid wives for generations.
  Bride-price in livestock ties herds together; the levirate (a widow marries
  her husband's brother) keeps herd and children inside the group. Elite
  polygyny multiplies alliances; women ran the camp during campaigns and
  Töregene (1242–46) and Sorghaghtani Beki held real power in interregna.
- *Outsiders are absorbed by redistribution.* Conquered peoples (Tatars,
  Kereyid, Naiman) were split up across the decimal units and became
  "Mongols"; the Xiongnu employed Han defectors (the eunuch Zhonghang Yue as
  chief adviser; the general Li Ling); the Mongols spared and deported
  artisans by the tens of thousands (Samarkand 1220). The ethnonym follows
  the polity: "Türk" was the ruling clan's people before it named everyone
  they ruled; "Mongol" the same; "Kazakh" (roughly "free man / wanderer") is
  the name a *secession* took when Janibek and Kerei led their followers away
  from Abu'l-Khayr's Uzbeks in the 1450s — a political act that became an
  ethnicity. Genealogies were rewritten to match (Golden, Sneath).
- *Exit is always available.* A household that disagrees moves to another
  leader's camp. This is the constraint every leader lives under and the
  reason legitimacy must be re-earned (1.3).

**Mechanism:** small mobile groups must recruit or die; consent-based kinship
lets them absorb anyone useful, and the exit option disciplines leaders.

### 1.5 The relationship with sedentary states

**Sedentary assumption:** the steppe is outside; the border keeps it out;
relations are war or submission.

**Steppe practice — what nomads needed and did:**

- *Needs.* Grain (a supplement, not a staple — Beckwith is right that raids
  targeted livestock and people), iron (the Han banned iron exports for a
  reason), textiles, later tea; and above all prestige goods for
  redistribution.
- *Raid-or-trade.* Jagchid and Symons documented the pattern across two
  millennia: when markets are open, peace; when closed, raids. Altan Khan
  asked the Ming for horse fairs in 1551; the Ming granted two, then cancelled
  them when he asked to trade sheep and cattle for beans and grain; the raids
  resumed (he had already looted the suburbs of Beijing in 1550). The 1571
  settlement — title of Prince of Shunyi plus horse markets — bought decades of
  quiet on that frontier. Waldron's *Great Wall* argues the Ming walls were
  built precisely because the court refused the cheaper option of trade.
- *Barfield's outer frontier strategy* (Xiongnu template): (1) violent raids
  to terrify the court; (2) alternating war and peace to raise the subsidy and
  trade concessions; (3) deliberate refusal to occupy Chinese land — a nomad
  who takes land becomes taxable and loses the threat; (4) the chanyu
  redistributes the take to hold the confederacy.
- *Numbers.* Heqin (198 BC): a princess plus annual silk, wine, and grain.
  After Huhanye's submission (51 BC) the Han gave 5 kg gold, 200,000 cash, 77
  suits, 8,000 bolts of silk, ~1,500 kg silk floss, and 34,000 hu (~680,000 L)
  of grain; by 1 BC the silk had risen to 30,000 bolts, 30,000 jin of floss
  and 370 suits (Yü Ying-shih's table). Submission was renewed only as long
  as each visit brought more than the last. The Tang paid the Uyghurs 40
  bolts of silk per horse after 762 (up from 25); the *Tang* sources claim
  100,000 horses a year, which Sinor rejects as impossible; ~7,500 horses for
  300,000 bolts is one attested annual figure, and the Tang fell into arrears.
  The alternative was war: Han Wudi's campaigns (133–119 BC) lost over 100,000
  of the 140,000 horses sent in 119 BC and forced the state salt-and-iron
  monopolies (119–117 BC) to pay for it.
- *Asymmetry.* Han census of AD 2: 59.6 million. Xiongnu estimates run
  0.5–1.5 million. The Mongol army of 1227 was ~129,000 (Rashid al-Din) from a
  Mongol population under a million, against a Song China of ~100 million.
  The steppe was never a demographic peer; it was a mobilisation peer.
- *How nomads managed states.* Hostage princes at both courts; supplying
  cavalry to a dynasty in trouble (the Uyghurs retook Chang'an and Luoyang for
  the Tang in 757 and 762, then billed them in silk for a century); playing
  two states (the Zunghars between Russia and the Qing); court factions and
  marriage; and the steady use of the threat rather than the act.

**Sedentary management tools, in rising order of effectiveness:**

1. *Subsidy and marriage* (heqin; the "tribute system" in which the nomad
   "submits" and receives gifts worth more than the tribute). Cheap, but the
   price ratchets.
2. *Walls and forts* (Qin/Han, Ming; Russian Orenburg, Irtysh, and Siberian
   lines, 1716–1740s). Lattimore: the wall keeps the Chinese *in* as much as
   nomads out — it stops the frontier population defecting to a cheaper way
   of life. Di Cosmo: walls were also forward bases for horse acquisition.
3. *Divide and rule* (Han "use barbarians to control barbarians"; Russian
   sponsorship of rival Kazakh sultans; Qing patronage of Khalkha against
   Zunghars).
4. *Fix them to the land* — the only method that ended the problem. See 1.9.

### 1.6 Warfare and mobility

**Sedentary assumption:** military power is numbers, walls, and supply
trains; you win by taking and holding places.

**Steppe practice:**

- *Every adult male is already a soldier*, already mounted, already supplied
  with the composite bow and 3–5 remounts (5–7 on campaign; some sources say
  more). Mobilisation ratio approached 1 in 5 of the population.
- *Speed.* ~95 km/day sustained; up to ~160 km/day in the 1241 Hungarian
  campaign. The *yam* relay had stations every 30–65 km with hundreds of horses
  each; couriers made 300–400 km/day. No pre-modern sedentary army moved
  faster than ~25 km/day.
- *No supply train.* The army drove its herds and drank mares' milk; the
  season chose the campaign (autumn after the horses fattened; winter for
  frozen rivers as roads in Rus' 1237–38).
- *Refusal of battle.* The Scythians against Darius (513 BC): retreat, fill
  the wells, burn the grass, and let the invader starve. Every steppe war
  against a sedentary invader was the same shape: the invader finds nothing
  to hit and runs out of grain in weeks. Han and Qing campaigns succeeded only
  when they solved that (see 1.9).
- *Nomad weaknesses.* Sieges (until Chinese and Persian engineers were
  hired); horse attrition in wet, hot, or pasture-poor country (Hungary,
  Syria 1260); and the demographic cap — a steppe army lost was a generation
  lost.

**Mechanism:** the nomad's army *was* his economy on the move; the sedentary
army was a cost centre detached from its economy, with a leash whose length
was set by grain.

### 1.7 Religion and cosmology

**Sedentary assumption:** religion attaches to temples, priesthoods, and holy
places; it is a fixed institution.

**Steppe practice:**

- *Tengri (sky), Etügen/Yer-Sub (earth-water), ancestor spirits.* No temples;
  the sky is the one sacred thing that comes with you; earth-water is
  wherever you are. Shamans (*böö*, *qam*) are individuals, not an
  establishment. Ovoo cairns mark passes and routes — sacred geography is a
  network, not a centre. There are fixed holy places (Burkhan Khaldun, the
  Ötüken forest as the Türk "seat"), but they are where the ruling clan
  originated, not where the god lives.
- *Mandate = success.* Heaven's favour is read off outcomes (1.3), which makes
  religion an instrument of legitimacy that requires no clergy.
- *Pluralism as policy.* Möngke to Rubruck (1254): God gave the hand several
  fingers. Empires adopted world religions as diplomatic and commercial
  moves: Uyghurs → Manichaeism (762, via Sogdian merchants); Khazars →
  Judaism; the Golden Horde and Chaghatai → Islam; Altan Khan → Tibetan
  Buddhism (1578, coining "Dalai Lama"). Each purchase brought literate
  administrators and a trade network.
- *Adopting a sedentary religion is a step toward being sedentarised.* Qing
  patronage of Buddhism built monasteries that fixed population and capital
  in place; by 1900 a large fraction of Mongol men (commonly estimated at a
  third or more) were lamas.

### 1.8 Law and dispute resolution

**Sedentary assumption:** law needs courts, prisons, and a state monopoly on
force.

**Steppe practice:**

- *Judges by reputation, chosen by the parties.* The Kazakh *biy* held no
  office; parties went to him because he knew the *adat* and was known to be
  fair, and paid a fee (*bilik*, roughly a tenth). A biy whose judgments were
  not accepted stopped being one.
- *Penalties are transfers, not confinement.* The *Zheti Zhargy* (Tauke Khan,
  c. 1680): *kun* of 1,000 sheep for killing a common man, 500 for a woman,
  seven men's worth for a sultan; *aiyp* fines in livestock for lesser
  offences (nine sheep for insulting a sultan). No prisons; the alternatives
  were fine, restitution, exile, or death.
- *Collective liability.* The killer's kin pay the kun; if they refuse, the
  victim's kin have a licensed feud. Groups therefore police their own
  members.
- *Hospitality is enforceable.* Refusing a traveller food and shelter
  (*konakasy*) was actionable before a biy — in a mobile society the duty to
  host is infrastructure, not manners.
- *The Mongol Yasa.* Shigi Qutuqu appointed *jarghuchi* (judge) in 1206 with
  the "blue book" registry of decisions and household assignments;
  punishments were harsh (death for horse theft, for harbouring runaway
  slaves) and graded by rank (bloodless execution for nobles). Whether a
  written code ever existed is disputed (Morgan); the fragments come from
  Juvaini, Rashid al-Din, and Maqrizi.
- *The final sanction is exit.* A group that rejects a judgment moves; a
  member expelled from the group is, on the steppe, dead. Enforcement rests
  on the fact that nobody can survive alone.

### 1.9 What closed the steppe

**Sedentary assumption (and the popular one):** gunpowder ended the nomad.

**What the evidence says:**

- *Guns mattered least, and late.* The Zunghars had muskets and cast their
  own cannon with the help of the Swedish prisoner Renat (1716–33). The
  Russian frontier literature notes that Khodarkovsky's account of the
  Russian advance barely needs to mention gunpowder.
- *Logistics closed it.* Perdue (*China Marches West*): Qing victory over the
  Zunghars (1690s–1757) rested on grain magazines, camel supply trains,
  military-agricultural colonies (*tuntian*), and the willingness to spend for
  decades. The Kangxi emperor's 1696 campaign that beat Galdan at Jao Modo
  solved the Scythian problem by carrying the grain.
- *Partition closed it.* The treaties of Nerchinsk (1689) and Kyakhta (1727)
  drew a Russia–Qing border across the steppe and — the decisive clause —
  committed each side to return the other's fugitives. When both neighbours
  agree not to host your refugees, the exit option that disciplined every
  steppe leader is gone. The Zunghars were crushed between two states that
  had agreed on the map.
- *Disease and extermination.* Of the Zunghars, an estimated 70–80 %
  (420,000–480,000 people) died in 1755–58 — ~40 % of smallpox, ~30 % killed by
  the Qing army, the rest fled. The last mobile-nomad empire ended as a
  demographic event.
- *Fixing people to land.* The Qing banner system divided the Khalkha into
  roughly 80–90 *khoshuu* with fixed boundaries, hereditary salaried *jasagh*,
  and a prohibition on movement across banner lines; migration became an
  administrative offence. Russia: Speransky's 1822 statute abolished the
  Middle Horde khanate; the 1868 Provisional Statute declared Kazakh land
  state property; peasant colonisation removed 4 million acres from Kazakh
  use 1893–1905, 17 million 1906–12, 45 million by 1917; ~2 million Slavic
  settlers had arrived by 1914, each male head granted ~30 desyatinas and 15
  years' tax exemption. Routes cut into allotments cannot be migrated along.
- *Debt as a leash.* Nineteenth-century Mongol banners carried collective
  debts to Shanxi merchant houses licensed by the Qing; nobles paid stipends
  by Beijing; Kazakh sultans became salaried officials. The aristocracy that
  Sneath describes was bought.
- *The end state.* Soviet collectivisation 1929–33 seized the herds and
  forced settlement; 1.5–2.3 million Kazakhs died (38–42 % of the people),
  herds fell > 80 %, and nomadism never returned because there were no animals
  to resume it with. The Torghut (Kalmyk) exodus of 1771 — ~170,000 left the
  Volga in January, perhaps a third to half reached the Ili in July, the
  fifth left behind on the west bank became the Russian Kalmyks — was the last
  time a whole people tried to move out of one empire's reach into another's.
  The Qing settled the survivors in fixed banners.

**Generalised mechanism for closing a mobile society:** (1) make the invader's
logistics independent of the land; (2) get the *neighbours* to agree on a
border and an extradition clause; (3) register the people and fix each group to
an allotment; (4) cut routes into parcels and give the parcels to farmers; (5)
replace the leader's external revenue with a stipend and his followers'
autonomy with debt; (6) let disease and demography finish it. Note that steps
2–5 are administrative, not military. Beckwith adds a sixth cause the others
underweight: the sea route killed the Silk Road economy the steppe lived
inside, so there was less to extract.

## 3. Implications for mechanics

Each item: **inverted assumption → what the steppe did → candidate mechanic
(act) → where the belt analogy breaks.**

1. **Wealth accumulates → wealth is volatile and is spent on obligations.**
   *Mechanic (Act 1→2):* assets are fleet, volatiles, and capability, all
   depletable or destructible in one turn (a solar-particle event, a reactor
   failure, a pressure loss = the zud; calibrate to 15–25 % loss events at a
   1-in-5-to-10-turn frequency). Add a *social ledger*: households and crews
   can lend surplus (stock-loan analog: propellant, spares, a crew member) and
   the loan is recorded as obligation; obligations survive disasters and are
   the only insurance. "Rich" is rendered as obligations owed to you plus
   followers, not tonnage. *Break:* asteroid volatiles do not regrow like
   grass; a depleted node stays depleted, so the belt is closer to a hunting
   or whaling economy than a grazing one. Model *claims* as renewable
   (rotational access) even though the ore is not.

2. **Estates are inherited whole → inheritance is pre-mortem and fissive.**
   *Mechanic (Act 1→2):* when a household or crew forms (marriage,
   partnership), it draws down a share of the parent's fleet/volatiles and
   becomes a new unit; the "youngest keeps the hearth." Growth of the colony is
   growth in the number of mobile units, and each fission is a seed for a later
   rival or ally. *Break:* a spacecraft is lumpy; a herd divides, a ship does
   not. Fission needs a "hull threshold" — you cannot split below one viable
   vessel, which makes fission rarer and more political than on the steppe.

3. **Territory is area with borders → territory is nodes-at-seasons and the
   routes between them.** *Mechanic (Act 2→3):* claims are on bodies at
   orbital phases / launch windows, not on regions; the "winter camp" is the
   water-rich body you must hold, everything else is opportunistic. Defence
   budget attaches to chokepoints (depots, low-Δv transfer nodes) and there is
   no perimeter to hold. Use the time-varying graph from report 01 directly:
   an edge that is cheap this year and dear next year *is* the seasonal
   pasture. *Break:* movement was nearly free on the steppe (the horse eats
   the pasture); in the belt each move costs reaction mass. So belt mobility
   is burst mobility — pay Δv per migration, coast free between. A group that
   has spent its Δv is sedentarised by default. That inverts the steppe rule:
   the *poor* belter is the *immobile* belter.

4. **Sovereignty over land → sovereignty over people (ulus).** *Mechanic
   (Act 2→3):* a polity is a list of households/crews with their fleets; the
   map is derived. A leader's power is the count that follows when he moves;
   defection is a household changing the leader field and leaving. No
   "control" of a body without people at it. *Break:* none in principle; the
   belt strengthens it, since population is tiny and every hull counts.

5. **Legitimacy from office → legitimacy from success (qut).** *Mechanic
   (Act 2→3):* a leader has a *fortune* stat updated by outcomes (deliveries,
   deals, disasters survived); below a threshold, followers defect in cascades.
   Every leader death or incapacity triggers a *quriltai* turn: eligible
   candidates (of the founding lineage, or of whoever the culture recognises)
   compete by coalition size; the game does not offer primogeniture unless the
   culture has drifted toward it. *Break:* none; if anything a small colony
   makes it sharper.

6. **States grow by conquering land and taxing it → confederacies grow by
   coalition and are sized to external revenue.** *Mechanic (Act 3):*
   confederation stability = (external revenue redistributed) / (number of
   member leaders × their expectation). Revenue comes from the inner system
   (volatile sales, tolls, subsidies). When the inner system is rich and
   unified the belt confederacy can be held; when it fragments, the
   confederacy dissolves (Barfield's cycle) unless the player has converted it
   into an administration (Chinggis's decimal breakup: mixed-crew units,
   hostage-officers in a guard, appointed over born captains). Offer that
   conversion as an expensive, unpopular, decisive option. *Break:* Beckwith's
   objection applies with force — if the belt's own internal trade is large
   enough, the confederacy is self-financing and the cycle weakens; make the
   dependency ratio a parameter the capability ladder (report 03) moves.

7. **Identity is birth → identity is membership, made by oath, adoption,
   marriage, and redistribution.** *Mechanic (Act 2):* kinship is a relation
   the player creates (crew oath = anda; adoption; marriage alliance with
   another group = quda); newcomers and defectors are split across crews
   rather than kept as a bloc; the culture's ethnonym ("Voidborn") is a
   political label that people can adopt or drop. Birthplace-as-culture-seed
   (NOTES) should be *overridable* by oath and time in the group. *Break:*
   none; the Bajau/Polynesian material will say the same.

8. **The goal is self-sufficiency → dependence is structural; the art is
   managing it (raid-or-trade, outer frontier strategy).** *Mechanic (Act
   1→3):* sponsor and inner-system relations as an escalation ladder — request
   markets; withhold volatiles; seize inbound cargo; alternate cooperation and
   defiance to ratchet the subsidy (Han silk ×3.75 in 50 years); *never take
   inner-system territory*. Open markets → peace turns; closed markets →
   raid/piracy turns. The sponsor AI's toolkit is the sedentary one: subsidy,
   marriage-equivalents (secondments, hostages), divide-and-rule between belt
   groups, and finally fixing you to a body. *Break:* on the steppe a raid
   reached undefended surface wealth in days and left before the army came.
   In space nothing is stealthy and approach takes months, so the raid is
   telegraphed and the target is *shipping*, not the homeland — piracy and
   embargo, not the Beijing suburbs. The nomad's threat was seizure; the
   belter's is denial.

9. **Strength is numbers and walls → strength is mobility and the invader's
   inability to feed itself in your space.** *Mechanic (Act 3):* an
   inner-system expedition into the belt pays logistics per turn (propellant,
   consumables, crew-time) and finds empty rock if the defenders relocate;
   the "Scythian defence" is a legal move (refuse contact, fill the wells =
   move or spoil the depots). The counter the sedentary AI can buy is exactly
   Perdue's: depots and magazines along the route, decades of spend, and
   colonies at the chokepoints. *Break:* the belt has no demographic
   mobilisation advantage (1-in-5 armed riders); its asset is position (Δv
   from belt to cislunar is cheaper than from Earth's surface) and volatiles.
   Do not give belters a cavalry; give them the ability to not be there.

10. **Religion lives in temples → religion is portable (sky), route-marked
    (ovoo), ancestor-based, and validated by success.** *Mechanic (Act 2):*
    cosmology drift as a set of stats (sky/sun-centred vs Earth-centred;
    ancestor ships as sacred; ovoo-like markers at transfer nodes that carry
    route knowledge). Adopting an inner-system religion is a diplomatic move
    with a sedentarising side-effect (fixed institutions, resident clergy) —
    the Uyghur/Manichaean and Mongol/Buddhist pattern. *Break:* the steppe sky
    is a dome overhead; in the belt "sky" is all there is and the Sun is a
    fixed directional power source. Expect solar rather than celestial-dome
    cosmology; the "eternal blue sky" may become "the light."

11. **Law needs courts and prisons → law is chosen arbiters, fines in
    livestock, collective liability, enforceable hospitality, and exit.**
    *Mechanic (Act 2):* disputes go to an arbiter the parties choose (surface
    it through the advisor ring; arbiters are people with reputations);
    penalties are transfers (volatiles, crew-time, a share of a claim);
    the offender's crew is jointly liable; refusing dock/refuel/air to a
    transient is itself an offence; a losing party's ultimate move is to
    leave. No prisons — a confined crew member is dead weight on a ship. *Break:*
    exit is slow and Δv-limited, so the exit sanction is weaker and feud
    correspondingly stronger; expect more kun-style pricing, not less.

12. **Frontiers are closed by better weapons → closed by logistics,
    partition-with-extradition, registration, allotment, stipends and debt.**
    *Mechanic (Act 3):* the inner-system AI's *enclosure playbook* as a
    scripted strategy: (a) build depots so its ships do not depend on belt
    water; (b) get the *other* inner-system powers to agree on a belt
    partition and to return each other's fugitives (Nerchinsk/Kyakhta); (c)
    mandate transponders and registries (the banner census); (d) grant fixed
    claims that cannot be left (banner boundaries) and then lease the routes
    between them to newcomers (peasant colonisation); (e) put belt leaders on
    salary and belt groups on credit. Each step is administrative, cheap for
    the inner system, and reversible by the player only early. The player's
    counters are the historical ones: keep two rival sponsors bidding, refuse
    fixed claims, avoid debt, keep a reservoir of unregistered fleet. *Break:*
    walls are impossible in space, and disease is an unlikely finisher; but the
    "no one will host your refugees" clause is if anything stronger, because
    there is nowhere else with air.

13. **Conquerors come from the core → they come from the margin (Lattimore's
    marginal zone, Barfield's inner frontier strategy).** *Mechanic (Act 3):*
    the group best placed to confederate the belt is not the deepest-belt
    Voidborn but a mixed group at the edge (NEA or Mars-crossing orbits) with
    a partly sedentary economy, inner-system contacts, and the belt's mobility.
    Seed this in Act 1 by making the player's birthplace/orbit a marginal one,
    or by placing such a rival. *Break:* none.

14. **Time.** Coalitions formed in ~25 years and empires dissolved in ~50;
    succession crises every generation. If a turn is a month, Act 3 is
    300–600 turns; if a year, 25–50. The steppe suggests Act 3 should be
    *short and unstable*, ending in either administrative conversion (item 6)
    or dissolution, not in a stable "belt nation."

15. **Two model-choice parameters, not decisions.** Expose (a) the
    *dependency ratio* (Barfield ↔ Beckwith) as an output of the capability
    ladder, and (b) the *aristocracy weight* (Sneath ↔ tribe model) as a
    culture-drift variable: does authority rest in hereditary households with
    assigned followers, or in consent and charisma alone? Both were true on the
    steppe at different times and scales; the sim should let the player's
    choices move the needle.

## 4. Open questions

- **Is there a renewable "grass" in the belt?** Solar power is renewable;
  volatiles are not. The steppe analogy assumes the base resource regrows and
  only the animals die. If the belt's base resource depletes, the correct
  analogy may be nomadic *hunting* or whaling fleets, with a much stronger
  drive toward territorial claims. Report 03's closure numbers decide this.
- **Mobilisation.** The steppe's political leverage was that a million people
  could put 100,000+ riders in the field. The belt has no such mass. What is
  the belter's equivalent threat that is credible, telegraphed months ahead,
  and still works? Denial of volatiles is the obvious answer but needs a
  demand-side model (report 04) to be a real lever.
- **How much does Δv-limited exit weaken the whole model?** Exit disciplines
  leaders, enforces law, and defeats invaders. If moving costs a large share
  of a household's fleet capability, all three mechanisms weaken together.
  Needs a number from report 02: what fraction of a group's Δv budget does one
  "migration" consume?
- **Sneath vs tribe: which is the default culture state at the end of Act 1?**
  A sponsored outpost arrives with a corporate hierarchy (Sneath-like:
  assigned households, appointed heads). Does drift toward Voidborn move it
  toward consent-based charisma, or does the hierarchy persist as a "headless
  state" after the sponsor goes? Design decision; the history supports both.
- **Turn length and act pacing** (item 14). The steppe timescales argue for
  Act 3 being shorter than Act 2.
- **Did steppe empires exist without a rich neighbour?** Barfield says no,
  Di Cosmo says the Xiongnu formed from a military crisis, Beckwith says trade
  suffices. The sim's Act 2 ("cut off") is a direct test of this: with no
  inner-system revenue, can any belt polity larger than a household coalition
  form? The evidence says only a small, unstable one — which is probably the
  right Act 2 experience.
- **Blood price calibration.** Kun of 1,000 sheep is 3–10 household herds. What
  is the belt's equivalent unit — crew-years, tonnes of water, a hull share?
