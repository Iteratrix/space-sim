# 08 — Life in the can: small-group dynamics in sealed, isolated, confined environments

Status: complete first pass. Sections per BRIEF.md. Sources are numbered [n] and listed in section 5.

A note on method. The ICE (isolated, confined, extreme) literature is small, mostly N=6 to N=90 per study, and heavily anecdotal before ~1990. Where I say "documented" below I mean a study with a stated sample and instrument, not a memoir or a press story. Where the only evidence is a memoir or a news report I say so. Several popular claims (the "third-quarter phenomenon" as a law, Dunbar's 150, the Vostok chess-axe murder) do not survive contact with the primary studies, and I flag them.

## 1. Findings

### 1.1 What the ICE literature actually shows

**Baseline: symptom rates, psychiatric incidence.** The best-sampled cohort is Palinkas's US Antarctic Program work. In the 1989 McMurdo winter crew (N=119), 64.1% reported sleep problems, 62.1% feeling depressed, 47.6% more irritable than usual, 51.5% concentration/memory trouble [1]. In debriefings of 313 winter-overs at McMurdo and South Pole 1994–97, 12.5% met DSM-IV criteria for a disorder; weighted for participation bias the incidence was **5.2% per winter**. Mood disorders 30.2% of diagnoses, adjustment 27.9%, sleep 20.9%, personality 11.6%, substance 9.3%. Depressive symptoms were associated with female gender, military occupation, *which station*, and *which year* — the authors conclude the social environment is a stronger determinant than the physical one [2]. Every one of those people had passed psychiatric screening [1]. Palinkas and Suedfeld's Lancet review gives the same ~5% figure and names three overlapping seasonal syndromes: winter-over syndrome, polar T3 syndrome (thyroid-mediated cognitive dulling), and subsyndromal SAD [3].

**Time course — the third-quarter phenomenon (TQP) is real but not a law.** Bechtel & Berning (1991) proposed that mood and morale dip in the third quarter of a confinement *regardless of its length*, because the crew knows the halfway point has passed and an equally long stretch remains (temporal expectancy) [4]. Evidence:

- *For:* Stuster's content analysis of nine French circumpolar expedition leaders' and physicians' journals found "substantial evidence of a third quarter phenomenon in all expeditions" — and, unexpectedly, that *shorter* missions (69–180 d) produced more negative entries than longer ones (230–363 d) [5]. Sandal (2000): land-based Antarctic personnel (N=18) showed a marked coping decline around the third quarter tied to interpersonal stress; ship-based personnel (N=19) did not [6]. Sandal et al. (2018) at Concordia (two crews, N=27, ~10 months): *all* coping strategies — problem-solving, avoidance, comforting cognitions — dropped around midwinter; positive affect hit its minimum; negative affect did *not* rise. They call it "psychological hibernation": flattening, not distress [7]. Palinkas & Houseal (2000, N=87, three stations): South Pole mood improved March→August then tension and fatigue rose August→October; McMurdo similar; Palmer (the smallest, mildest station) showed no change at all [8].
- *Against:* Kanas's Shuttle/Mir study (13 crew, 58 mission control) found "no evidence suggesting a 3rd quarter effect among crewmembers on any of the 21 subscales" [9]; the ISS replication (17 crew, 128 MCC) again found no quarter effect [10]. Mars-500 (N=6, 520 d): depression scores were higher in the second half, but no quarter signature; two of six crew accounted for the pattern [11]. HI-SEAS (three crews, 8–12 months): biomarkers and self-report suggest trouble begins *after the halfway point* and then oscillates until the end, "not a renewed outlook at the end as described in 3rd quarter theory" [12]. SIRIUS-21 hindrance ties were highest early and late and *lowest* mid-mission [13].

My reading: a post-midpoint slump exists in fixed-term land deployments where the end date is known and the environment is monotonous; it is absent or washed out in crews with heavy task loads (spaceflight), on ships (which move), and it varies more between crews than between quarters. The mechanism is *expectancy*, which matters enormously for this game: **a population with no return date has no third quarter.** That is the difference between Act 1 (contract crews with rotation dates) and Act 2 (Voidborn, open-ended).

**Individual heterogeneity dominates aggregate mood.** This is the most robust single finding and the one pop-psych ignores. Mars-500: two crewmembers accounted for 85% of all reported conflicts; one had depressive symptoms in 93% of mission weeks; another developed chronic sleep-onset insomnia and made 64% of errors of omission; two crewmembers showed no problems at all; **55% of variance in self-report outcomes was stable between-person difference** (ICC 0.60–0.79) [11]. Wood et al. (1999), two six-man 100-day Antarctic traverses: "most of the psychological discomfort ... appeared to be within the individual or between individuals. They did not affect all members of the group equally" [14]. Kanas: critical incidents "were from a relatively small number of subjects" [9]. Design consequence: a scalar "morale" is the wrong abstraction. The sim needs persons with stable traits and dyadic relations; group-level indices should be *derived*.

**Displacement of tension onto outsiders is the most replicated group-level effect.** Kanas found "strong evidence" that crews displace tension and negative emotion onto mission control, and mission control onto management, on Mir [9] and again on ISS (all six subscales) [10]. Mars-500: 41 recorded conflicts with mission control vs 8 among crew — five to one — peaking during the 30-day "Mars surface" phase [11]. Soviet-era space-cabin simulators of 30, 60 and 90 days reported displacement to outside observers in every study [1]. The Skylab 4 "strike" (Dec 1973) was a crew falling behind an over-packed schedule, cutting briefings, missing one comm pass; the ground responded with a scheduling "sensitivity session", and productivity then exceeded Skylab 3 [15]. IBMP's communication studies name the complementary process "detachment"/autonomization: crew–MCC message volume falls over a long confinement, briefly spiking at the landing simulation, and crew communication styles converge [16]. Kanas and Palinkas both note displacement is *adaptive*: a remote target absorbs aggression that would otherwise land on a crewmate, and a shared enemy unites the crew [1]. Corollary for the game: **remove the sponsor and the displacement target vanishes; conflict turns inward.**

**Network structure, not mood, predicts which crews go bad.** Johnson, Boster & Palinkas studied three South Pole winters with pile-sort network data. Crews with a *core-periphery* structure (a unitary core, semi-periphery, periphery) had lower tension, depression and anger all winter than a crew with a *clique* structure (three leisure-space subgroups plus isolates); a hybrid was in between. Coherence was associated with **group consensus on who filled critical informal roles** — above all the instrumental leader, and also an expressive leader; factionalization went with absence of that consensus [17][1]. The station manager of the clique crew "remained fairly isolated from the remainder of the crew and made few attempts to exercise authoritarian or participatory styles"; the core-periphery crew's manager was praised for varying his style [1]. SIRIUS-21 (N=6→5, 8 months, network surveys twice weekly, two non-isolated twin teams as controls): before the day-32 loss of a crewmember to injury, leadership was "shared-coordinated" (two leaders relying on each other); afterwards it alternated among hierarchical (67% of measurements), shared-fragmented (27%) and absent (7%) and "never recovered"; two viability subgroups formed that persisted to the end — *not* along national or gender lines [13]. Sandal's ESA chamber studies (4 and 9 weeks): "negative relations established early in the isolation remained stable over time"; dominance and task motivation drove compatibility [18].

**Biosphere 2 (8 people, 2 years, 1991–93).** The crew split into two factions of four before the halfway point; Poynter: "people who had been intimate friends had become implacable enemies, barely on speaking terms." The nominal issue was research priorities vs closure integrity; the stressors were chronic hunger (average 16% body-weight loss), oxygen falling from 20.9% to 14.5% over 16 months, and CO₂ scrubbing that contradicted the project's public claims. Nelson, Gray & Allen's retrospective adds the decisive ingredient: "these conflicts were exacerbated by *external power struggles which enlisted support of those inside*" [19][20]. The crew nonetheless stayed "on task" — the split did not stop the work — and in 1994 two Mission-1 alumni broke seals on the successor mission during a management takeover [20]. The seed here is not "8 people split 4/4"; it is "an outside faction fight recruits inside allies and the crew mirrors it."

**Historical polar expeditions** supply the extreme tail. *Belgica* (1898–99, 19 men, five nationalities, 13 months in the ice, first Antarctic winter-over): two deaths, at least one crewmember (Tollefsen) committed to an asylum on return, commander and captain both incapacitated so the doctor (Cook) and first mate (Amundsen) ran the ship; Cook's countermeasures were forced exercise, a fire-light "baking" treatment, and raw penguin/seal meat against scurvy — and the first Midwinter feast [21]. *Jeannette* (1879–81, 33 men, ~2 years in Arctic ice): discipline held by formal reprimand and suspension of one scientist; 20 of 33 died on the retreat [22]. *Ross Sea party* (1915–17, 10 men stranded 20 months): Shackleton left command ambiguous between Mackintosh and Joyce; the party split by sledging method; de facto leadership migrated to Joyce and Richards as Mackintosh weakened; three died, one buried in the ice, two lost through thin ice [23]. The pattern across all three: **ambiguous or incapacitated formal authority is the precondition for a split; a competent informal leader (usually the doctor or the most experienced hand) is what saves the group.**

**Submarines** are the one ICE setting where sleep and mood mostly hold, because the schedule is dense and the crew is large and hierarchical. On a 70-day French SSBN patrol (N=19) polysomnography at day 21 vs 51 showed no change in sleep length or quality, cortisol, anxiety or mood [24]. But the 18-hour watch cycle on US boats does not entrain: melatonin free-ran at 24.35 h (N=12) [25]. Sandal's comparison found submariners more competitive and less achievement-striving than Antarctic personnel [6]. Food is the recognized morale lever: US submarine food budgets are the highest per head in the military (press accounts, not a study).

### 1.2 Group size

**Dunbar's number is not a usable constant.** Lindenfors, Wartel & Lind (2021) re-ran the primate neocortex regressions with modern phylogenetic methods: point estimates of 69–109 (Bayesian) or 16–42 (GLS) with 95% CIs of 4–520 and 2–336 — "specifying any one number is futile" [26]. The oft-cited "Hutterite colonies split at 150" is a survey claim from Dunbar's own work with thin documentation; Bernard–Killworth network-scale-up estimates give ~290 (median 231) [27]. What survives is the *layered* structure (an inner ~5, ~15, ~50, ~150) as a description of how people allocate social time, and that governance form changes with size — which the ICE record documents directly, without needing a cognitive limit:

Two size-dependent mechanisms stand out. (1) **Fault tolerance of roles:** at N≤20 every critical role is one person deep; the doctor's appendicitis (Rogozov, 1961) or the station manager's stroke (South Pole, 2011) removes the function [35][36]. (2) **Escape from a harasser or an enemy:** at N=6 there is nowhere to go; at N=50 there are cliques to retreat into; at N=1,000 there are bars and a second shift. The harassment data show reporting collapses when the victim cannot physically get away and the reporting channel controls their next contract [33][37].

### 1.3 Leadership, authority, informal roles

- **Two leader functions, different timing.** Kanas's Mir simulator (135 d) and flight studies: crew cohesion tracks the commander's *supportive* role; mission control cohesion tracks both task and support roles [10][1]. Gunderson & Nelson's 1960s Navy station work: task leadership matters most early (establishing camp), supportive leadership later [1]. Nicholas & Penwell's profile of the effective ICE leader from four analog types: achievement-oriented, personally staked in the outcome, flexible (commands in crisis, delegates otherwise), works alongside subordinates, impartial between cliques, gives frequent recognition [1].
- **Status leveling.** When the commander cannot or will not provide support and an informal crewmember does, "lines of authority may alter and the mission commander may experience status leveling" [1]. This is the Ross Sea/Belgica pattern in a modern citation.
- **Shared leadership is fragile.** SIRIUS-21's coordinated two-leader structure collapsed after one loss and never re-formed [13].
- **Cultural/organizational style is a variable, not a constant.** Russian ground control was reported as more confrontational, kept shorter contact windows, and docked cosmonauts' pay for unperformed tasks; NASA over-trains, Russia trains on the job [1]. Cross-cultural Antarctic comparison (13 crews, 5 nations, N=217): Americans at South Pole got more anxious and fatigued over winter; Russians at Vostok got *less* depressed and anxious; Indians at Maitri less angry; individualist orientation went with *both* lower social support and lower negative mood [38].
- **The doctor.** At small stations the physician is the second pillar: the only medical capacity (Rogozov operated on himself with two helpers holding mirror and instruments [35]; Nielsen self-biopsied and self-treated breast cancer until an October evacuation [36]); the confidant (Cook on *Belgica*); often the morale officer. Halley's 2016 roster of 13 had the station leader doubling as magistrate [28]. Rodney Marks saw the doctor three times in 36 hours and died anyway — with no autopsy possible for six months, no jurisdiction willing to act, and a contractor that "advised that no report exists" [29].
- **Other informal roles** documented in the South Pole network work: instrumental leader, expressive leader, and the isolate/deviant; crews that could not agree who the expressive leader was fragmented [17].
- **Conflict style is stable and accommodating.** Three four-person crews before and after a 264-day simulation: Accommodating above the 75th percentile, Collaborating below the 25th, no shift with isolation [39]. Crews avoid confronting; grievances accumulate.

### 1.4 The failure modes

- **Stages and ostracism.** Palinkas (1992): stage 1 open interaction and social comparison; stage 2 subgroup formation by occupation, leisure, ideology, sometimes hardening into cliques; stage 3 coalescence around a social core "at the expense of certain individuals who refuse to adhere to group norms," who are ostracized. Station culture is a negotiated set of rules produced by this process [1][40].
- **Social support erodes even though it is nominally available.** McMurdo (N=235) and South Pole (N=77): availability of support unchanged over winter, satisfaction with it fell significantly, depressed mood rose; distress preceded advice-seeking and advice-seeking preceded more distress [41].
- **Romance, sex, harassment.** Mixed crews are recent (ANARE first women 1976) [37]. SFINCSS-99 (110-day ISS simulation, IBMP): a Russian commander forcibly kissed the Canadian participant at New Year, two Russians fought bloodily, the international module's hatch was sealed, a Japanese participant left; IBMP's project lead blamed her refusal for the "experiment's failure" [42]. AAD survey: 63% of women had received sexist remarks, 70% of those stayed silent; 44% had never had a female station or field leader [37]. In the 2022 USAP survey the mechanism is explicit: "if you're working with your harasser, you literally can't get away from them" and reporting ends careers [33]. Esperanza and Villa Las Estrellas exist partly *because* family postings were seen as a stabilizer (and a sovereignty claim) [30][43].
- **Alcohol.** 9.3% of Antarctic diagnoses are substance-related [2]; the NSF OIG 2015 audit found alcohol behind fights, indecent exposure, drunk-at-work and illicit brewing, and recommended breathalysers [31]; the 2018 Bellingshausen stabbing (welder stabbed by electrician, population 20) involved intoxication — the "spoiled book endings" motive was a press invention; charges were dropped after the victim forgave him [44]. Pitcairn's first decade: "alcoholism, murder, disease ... took the lives of most mutineers and Tahitian men" from a founding population of 27 [45]. Home-brewing appears everywhere alcohol is rationed.
- **Sleep, light, circadian.** 51 Australian winter-overs over six months: mean sleep 7.2 h but 41.4% of episodes <7 h, 19.1% <6 h; 34 of 50 had sleep at an abnormal circadian phase, 18.8% of all sleep episodes; misaligned nights were 6.1 h vs 7.3 h aligned; mood and performance worse near the melatonin peak [46]. Maitri (N=20): winter melatonin markedly higher, midwinter DASS scores higher, morning melatonin correlated with better sleep and cognition [47]. Mars-500: one man free-ran on a ~25 h day, one split his sleep; the crew spent 700 more hours in bed on the "return leg"; light exposure fell over the mission; hypokinesis progressed [48][11]. Submariners on 18-h watches do not entrain [25]. Lighting is a *capital good* with behavioural yield.
- **Boredom and workload.** In 657 Navy men over 8 stations (1963–74), boredom was inversely associated with task ability, emotional stability, social compatibility and overall performance; the *needs* for achievement, order, affection and efficiency were all *negative* predictors — frustrated by an environment that will not let you satisfy them [49][1]. Stuster's diaries rank issues: Group Interaction > Outside Communications > Workload > Recreation > Medical Support > Adjustment > Leadership > Event > Food [5]. ISS journals: Work, Outside Communications, Adjustment, Group Interaction = 61% of entries; overwork and underwork produce the same symptoms [50][1].
- **Privacy and territory.** Palinkas: territoriality becomes important; "social monotony" from the same faces; willingness to support others is counterbalanced by "fear of being burdened by the others' problems that are similar to one's own" [1]. Concordia's crew lost 4.5% body weight over a year with ad-libitum food — under-eating, not bad food [51].
- **Value drift.** Twelve ISS astronauts (4–7 months): hedonism and power rose early and fell later; benevolence and security dipped then recovered; perceived value incongruence predicted interpersonal tension on 7 of 8 values [52]. Mars-105 subgrouping formed on personal values [13].

### 1.5 Culture formation

- **Midwinter** began on *Belgica* in 1898 and is now "easily the biggest celebration of the year, surpassing Christmas" at every wintering station: a feast of hoarded delicacies, handmade secret-Santa gifts, greetings from heads of state (Kennedy 1961), electronic cards exchanged between all stations, breakfast in bed served by the base commander, BBC World Service messages to BAS bases [53]. Station-specific rites: the 300 Club (South Pole, since the 1950s: 200 °F sauna, run to the Pole at −100 °F), the Polar Plunge (now continent-wide), Signy's thin-ice race, the 48-Hour Film Festival (since 2006, all stations, five compulsory elements), the South Pole triple bill of *The Thing* after the last flight leaves [54][36]. Naval line-crossing (pollywog→shellback, King Neptune's court) is the maritime template: an initiation that marks who has "been there," with a documented history of hazing lethal enough to require reforms in the 1980s [55].
- **Dialect.** Antarctic English is documented in Hince's *Antarctic Dictionary*: "winter-over," "toasty/toast" (winter-dulled), "big eye" (insomnia), "freshies," "skua" (to scavenge), "beaker" (scientist), "boomerang" (aborted flight), "ice widow." Diaries and content analyses show emotional flattening in the vocabulary itself by late winter [7].
- **Convergence and autonomization.** SIRIUS-19: communication styles of all crew converged by the end, cohesion rose, contact with MCC fell [16]. Bulgaria's 33rd summer campaign (interviews pre N=28, post N=36): initial subgroups (veterans vs novices, science vs logistics) gave way to a "one-station" identity; the leader behaviours credited were transparent communication, mixed-group work assignment and active norm-setting; humour and routine buffered monotony dips [56].
- **What persists year to year:** at rotating stations, only the rituals, the slang, the physical traces (signed walls, the Pole marker) and the institutional memory of a few repeat winter-overs. Moscow found the mechanics who had wintered at Vostok before and could remember where a scrapped diesel was buried — that memory saved 19 people in 1982 [57].

### 1.6 Children

The literature is essentially empty; what exists is demographic. Esperanza (Argentina): a deliberate 1977–78 settlement programme flew a seven-months-pregnant woman in so that Emilio Palma would be born on the continent; at least eleven births 1978–83; ten families and two teachers in a winter population of 56; a school since 1978, a scout troop, a civil registry recording births and weddings, a radio station [43]. Villa Las Estrellas (Chile): 14 houses, school 1984–2018 (~300 children total, six in 2014), one doctor and nurse, two beds; residents including children are reported to require pre-emptive appendectomy — a policy rather than a study [30]. Longyearbyen is the opposite policy: no births, no burials, no welfare, the terminally ill and the unemployed must leave, and families leave when children reach 16–17 for want of upper schooling [34]. Ship-born children were common enough in the Royal Navy of the 18th–19th century that wives and children aboard are documented at Trafalgar (Stark, *Female Tars*), but nothing systematic exists on their development.

The Pitcairn case is the one hard data point on **what happens to children in a closed community without external oversight**: in 2004, charges against 13 men — "nearly a third of the male population and half of the island's adult males," including the sitting Mayor — for sexual offences against girls over decades, normalized across generations [45]. That is the failure mode to model: not "kids get bored," but norm drift in a sealed group where the people who would enforce a norm are the ones breaking it.

Open developmental questions the sim will have to take a position on: peer-group size (a cohort of 3 is not a school); who teaches; whether the birthplace seed (NOTES.md) should carry the *ritual* and *dialect* of the habitat as heritable culture; and what "consent to be here" means for someone who never signed the contract.

### 1.7 Death, illness, no evacuation

- **Incidence.** BAS 1986–95: cold injury 65.6 per 1,000 person-years was 2.5% of all new consultations, implying roughly 2.6 medical consultations per person-year, 78% of injuries recreational [58]. Herpes zoster in the 2014 USAP winter population of 204: 33.3 per 1,000 person-years vs 3.2 in the US population — stress immunosuppression is measurable [59]. A 2024 systematic review of Antarctic emergencies puts serious injury/poisoning at about 1 per 357 person-years (from the abstract as indexed; treat as order of magnitude) [60]. Antarctic winter medevacs are rare and dangerous; one Japanese series recorded 21 summer evacuations and 2 in winter across 11 stations [61]; the US TRAC2ES dataset shows 31 evacuations for 29 patients from McMurdo/Pole including behavioural health and pregnancy, most unrelated to the environment [62].
- **Surgery without a surgeon.** Rogozov's 1961 auto-appendectomy prompted Soviet policy changes [35]; the 2011 South Pole appendectomy was done by station doctors with winter-overs assisting [36]; the 2011 station manager's stroke went undiagnosed (no imaging) and NSF initially refused medevac [36]. The scoping review of 1904–2022 finds orthopaedics, dentistry and digestive surgery the commonest, with telemedicine now routine in the decision to operate or evacuate [63].
- **The body.** Marks (2000, South Pole, N=49): placed in a makeshift coffin in cold storage for ~6 months; autopsy in Christchurch found methanol; the inquest (2006) could not choose among accident, suicide and foul play; contractor and NSF did not cooperate with New Zealand police; witnesses feared for future employment [29]. Vostok 1982: fire killed one and destroyed all diesels at 4 am on 12 April; 19 survived 227 days on a resurrected scrapped engine and improvised stoves [57]. Ross Sea party: buried Spencer-Smith in the ice; two others simply vanished [23]. *Belgica* committed Danco to the sea through the ice [21]. Naval tradition: sailcloth, weights, feet-first; modern navies refrigerate and land the body. Longyearbyen banned burial when it found the 1918 flu dead had not decomposed [34].
- **Jurisdiction.** Under the Antarctic Treaty an accused is subject to their own country; the US enforces through the District of Hawaii and the Marshals; in practice contractors investigate themselves [44][31]. The Almirante Brown arson (1984): the station leader-doctor burned the base down rather than winter again [44].

### 1.8 Selection and training, and their limits

- **What predicts.** Palinkas et al. (2000), 657 Navy winter-overs: military service, low neuroticism, extraversion + conscientiousness, and *low* need for affection predicted performance; but everything together explained **9–17% of variance** [49]. Palinkas & Browner (1995, N=119): the only independent predictor of late-winter depression was pre-deployment depression [1]. Sandal: "the right stuff" (high instrumentality + expressivity) predicted adaptation in chambers [64]; "sociable introverts" do best; older people tolerate isolation better (Suedfeld) [65]. Helmreich's Right/Wrong/No Stuff clusters predict astronaut, aircrew and submariner performance [1].
- **What screening does not do.** "The experience of the United States Antarctic Program indicates that psychological screening has had little impact on the problem" of conflict (Palinkas 1990) [1]; 5.2% incidence after screening [2]; cosmonauts reported countermeasures ineffective after 30 days [1]. NASA's system is select-out (psychopathology) then select-in on ten suitability factors, with team-composition science (Bell et al. 2015) still immature [66].
- **The salutogenic side.** Suedfeld and Palinkas insist most people come out *stronger*: long-term health benefits, self-efficacy, and — the tell — they go back [3][40][65]. Coping resources acquired in one winter transfer.
- **The limit that matters for this game:** every study above is of volunteers who passed a screen. A self-reproducing habitat's second generation was not selected, did not volunteer, and inherits the norms of section 1.6. Nothing in the ICE literature covers that; the closest analogues are Pitcairn and Tristan.

## 3. Implications for mechanics

The brief asked for state variables and event-chain seeds. The evidence says: model *persons and ties*, derive group indices, and make the sponsor a structural part of the social system rather than an external economy.

### 3.1 State variables (replace "morale")

**Per person (stable, set at creation; birthplace/upbringing seeds them for the second generation):**
- `neuroticism` — the one trait that predicts anything [49]; scales symptom draws.
- `dominance` — two high-dominance people in one small crew is the classic incompatibility [1][18].
- `expressivity` / `instrumentality` — Helmreich's axes; high-both = "right stuff"; low-both = "no stuff" [1][64].
- `need_affection` — high is *bad* in ICE [49].
- `sociability_mode` — sociable-introvert (best) / extravert / true introvert [65].
- `conflict_mode` — accommodating (default for screened crews, stable) vs competing [39].
- `values` (a short vector: power, hedonism, benevolence, security, achievement) — incongruence between people drives tension [52][13].
- `experience_ice` — prior winters; confers coping resources and institutional memory [40][57].

**Per person (dynamic):**
- `strain` (one number is fine here, ~POMS total mood disturbance) with a trait-scaled random walk.
- `sleep_debt`, `circadian_misalignment` (0–1); driven by habitat lighting, watch schedule, and a per-person free-run susceptibility.
- `boredom` — inverse of meaningful task load; both under- and over-load raise strain [1][49].
- `support_satisfaction` — decays over isolation even at constant availability [41].
- `hunger` / body-mass drift — slow, but Biosphere 2 shows it is a faction accelerant [19].
- `substance_use` — gated by availability; contributes 9% of pathology and most violent incidents [2][31][44].
- `expectancy_horizon` — days to a known end date, or null for the Voidborn. **The third-quarter slump exists only when this is non-null** (section 1.1).

**Per dyad (directed):**
- `work_positive` ("who do you enjoy working with"), `hindrance` ("who makes tasks difficult"), `viability` ("would go on a 3-year mission with"), `leadership_reliance` — the four SIRIUS-21 relations [13]. Negative ties set early are sticky [18].

**Derived group indices (compute, do not store):**
- `coherence` — core-periphery vs factional (modularity of the positive/viability graph). The single best predictor of a bad winter [17].
- `role_consensus` — fraction of members agreeing on who is the instrumental leader / expressive leader [17]. Low consensus → clique formation.
- `leadership_structure` ∈ {shared-coordinated, hierarchical, shared-fragmented, absent} [13].
- `displacement_target_available` — is there an outside party (sponsor, mission control, another habitat) to blame? [9][10][11].
- `norm_strength` — how many negotiated rules exist and how many rituals have been held; grows with ritual events and shared crises; ostracism risk for deviants scales with it [40].
- `role_redundancy` — for each critical function (medic, power, life support, comms), how many people can do it. At N≤20 mostly 1 [28].
- `escape_capacity` — whether a person can avoid another person: number of separable social spaces / shifts. Drives harassment persistence and clique retreat [33][37].

### 3.2 Event-chain seeds

Each: trigger → chain → what the advisor ring can see → group-size note. Acts in brackets.

1. **The isolate.** [All] A person with high `need_affection` or low expressivity drifts to the periphery; `support_satisfaction` falls; advice-seeking rises and *increases* distress [41]; the core labels them deviant as norms harden [40]. Chain: withdrawal → error at work → scapegoating → (at N≤20) whole-group ostracism; (at N≥40) absorption into a marginal clique; (at N≥150) simply unnoticed. Resolution options: expressive leader intervenes; role reassignment; rotation out (Act 1 only).
2. **Third-quarter flattening.** [Act 1] With `expectancy_horizon` set, at 50–75% of the contract all coping strategies dim, positive affect bottoms, productivity slips, minor errors climb [7][5]. Not a crisis event — a *window* in which other seeds fire more easily. Disappears in Act 2 and is replaced by open-ended "no end in sight" dynamics: value drift [52] and settlement politics.
3. **Displacement onto the sponsor.** [Act 1] Conflicts route 5:1 toward mission control [11]. Sponsor tasking overload (Skylab 4) → crew cuts comms → sponsor escalates → crew unity rises, sponsor trust falls [15]. The player as leader chooses: absorb the sponsor's demands (crew blames you) or front the crew (sponsor docks pay/supplies — the Russian model [1]). **When the sponsor is lost at the Act 1→2 transition, `displacement_target_available` flips false and the hindrance graph re-routes inward within a few turns.** Substitute targets emerge: another habitat, "the inner system," the old guard.
4. **Outside faction fight recruits inside allies.** [Act 1, Act 3] Biosphere 2's actual mechanism [20]: a dispute among sponsors/investors (or in Act 3 among confederation partners) offers each faction inside allies; the crew mirrors the split; the split does not stop the work but is permanent and becomes the seed of Act 2 politics. Group size: at 8 it's 4/4; at 50 it's a clique alignment; at 250 it's a council vote.
5. **Loss of one and collapse of shared leadership.** [All] An injury, death or departure removes one of two coordinating leaders; leadership fragments (67% hierarchical, 27% fragmented, 7% absent) and viability subgroups form that persist to the end [13]. Group size: catastrophic at N≤8, structural at N≤20, absorbed at N≥50 if `role_consensus` is high.
6. **Ambiguous command.** [All] Two people each believe they hold authority (Ross Sea) [23]; the group splits by working method; formal leader weakens; de facto leadership migrates to the competent informal leader → `status_leveling` → the formal leader either accepts (cohesion recovers) or resists (faction). Player choice in the advisor ring: ratify the informal leader or reassert.
7. **Leader isolation.** [All] A station leader who withdraws produces a clique structure and no consensus on informal leaders [1]. Detectable by falling `leadership_reliance` in-degree. Interventions: participate in routine work, hold a ritual, appoint a deputy with expressive skill.
8. **The doctor goes down.** [All] Rogozov [35]; Nielsen [36]; at N≤20 `role_redundancy[medic]`=1 so the chain is: illness → self-treatment / telemedicine → either heroic success (a legend that enters `norm_strength`) or death → seed 10. Prophylactic policy options: mandatory appendectomy (Villa Las Estrellas), cross-training, second medic.
9. **Unexplained death.** [All] Marks [29]: a death with no autopsy possible for months; body in cold storage; rumours (accident? suicide? one of us?); investigating authority remote or absent; contractor/sponsor stonewalls; witnesses fear for contracts. Chain runs *until* evacuation of the body or, in Act 2, forever. Output: a permanent trust deficit toward the sponsor and one or two people who are never quite trusted again.
10. **Body handling as a norm-founding event.** [Act 2] The first death with no return: what the community does — ice burial, recycling of mass, a "vault" — becomes fixed custom and, given the Voidborn thesis, a defining cultural marker. Maritime precedent (burial at sea, feet first, sewn in) is the natural template; Longyearbyen's "no burials" is the sponsor-era counter-template [34].
11. **Power/heat loss with no relief.** [All] Vostok 1982 [57]: 1 dead, 227 days on a scrapped engine that only veterans remembered. Chain: crisis → who has `experience_ice`/institutional memory becomes instrumental leader regardless of rank → high cohesion for the duration → crash after relief. Salutogenic: survivors gain coping resources and become the next generation's veterans.
12. **The bar.** [All] Alcohol rationed → home brewing; drunk incidents dominate disciplinary events [31]; a stabbing among 20 [44]; sexual assault among 1,000 [33]. Policy levers: ration size, bar vs private drinking, breathalysers (enforcement requires someone willing to test — the OIG found only one of three contractors ever did). Trade-off: prohibition removes the one licensed social ritual; the sim should let both choices bite.
13. **Harassment with no escape.** [All] At N≤20 with a skewed sex ratio, harassment persists because the victim cannot leave and the reporting channel controls their next contract [33][37]; SFINCSS-99 shows the "lock the hatch" resolution — physically partitioning the crew — with the sponsor blaming the victim [42]. Consequence: loss of a whole subgroup's `viability` ties; in Act 1, contract non-renewal by the victim (loss of capability); in Act 2, exit is impossible and the norm either forms or fails (seed 17).
14. **Cliques by leisure space.** [N≥40] Three subgroups keyed to *where* people spend evenings [1]. The habitat's number of separable social spaces is therefore a design/capital input: too few → forced mixing and no escape; many → cliques harden. Coherence is best with one big core space plus retreat rooms.
15. **Sleep debt spiral.** [All] One crewmember's free-running rhythm puts them awake when the others sleep (20% of a 520-day mission for two Mars-500 crew [11]); they miss shared meals, drop out of the core, error rate rises, they become the isolate (seed 1). Levers: lighting capital, schedule design (a 24-h schedule entrains; an 18-h one does not [25]).
16. **Hunger as accelerant.** [Act 2] Closed-loop food shortfall → chronic hunger → Biosphere-style hostility [19]; at ad-lib but monotonous food, people simply under-eat (−4.5%/yr) [51]. Feasts (Midwinter) are the cheapest cohesion event in the record — cost is hoarded delicacies.
17. **Norm drift in a sealed second generation.** [Act 2→3] Pitcairn [45]: without external oversight, the people who would enforce a norm are the ones breaking it; abuse of the unselected young becomes customary. Slow chain (decades); surfaces in Act 3 when an outside authority (confederation, inner-system contact) arrives and half the adult men are implicated. The sim needs a "who polices the police" variable: an external audit relation (sponsor, treaty, another habitat) whose absence lets drift accumulate.
18. **Ritual founding.** [All] A first Midwinter (or its belt equivalent: aphelion, first-light, "crossing the line" at some orbital marker) fixes a date and a feast [53][21]; each repetition raises `norm_strength`; initiation rites (pollywog→shellback) create an in/out status that the second generation inherits by birth rather than ordeal — a documented source of resentment in navies [55].
19. **Cultural style mismatch with the sponsor.** [Act 1] Confrontational ground control, pay docking, short contact windows vs over-training and long contact [1]: choose a sponsor style at game start; it sets baseline displacement rate and the sponsor's tolerance for crew autonomization [16].
20. **Value drift over an open horizon.** [Act 2] Power/hedonism rise then fall; benevolence dips then recovers [52]; incongruence raises tension. For Voidborn crews the sim can let values *diverge* from the founding sponsor's, which is the mechanical content of "going Voidborn."
21. **Return of the veteran.** [All] Repeat winter-overs carry the memory that makes seeds 11 and 18 work; their loss (rotation, death) lowers `experience_ice` for the whole habitat. In Act 1 the sponsor rotates them out; in Act 2 they are the elders.

### 3.3 Group-size thresholds for the mechanics

- **≤8:** no derived group indices needed beyond the graph itself; every person visible in the advisor ring; one departure is a structural event (seed 5); splits are permanent; displacement needs an outside party.
- **12–20:** roles are 1-deep (seed 8); an isolate is 5–8% of the group; the station leader/doctor pair is the governance; the sim should model a *named* instrumental and expressive leader and check consensus each turn.
- **40–60:** cliques by space (seed 14) become possible; a periphery exists so isolates survive; alcohol/bar policy begins to matter; families and a registry become plausible (Esperanza).
- **80–150:** school, clinic, HR-like functions; harassment becomes statistical rather than personal; the advisor ring should start summarizing by clique/department rather than by person.
- **≥220:** elected council and communal-property rules (Tristan); a settlement, not a crew. Norm drift (seed 17) is the long game.
- **≥1,000:** policing, rations, shift populations; the ICE literature stops here and the cutoff-outpost report (07) should take over.

Do not hard-code 150; nothing in the evidence supports a discontinuity there [26].

### 3.4 Act-specific notes

- **Act 1:** contracts with end dates (expectancy), a sponsor to displace onto, rotation that removes veterans and isolates alike, screening that trims the tail (5% → maybe 3%) but changes nothing else. The player's real lever is *habitat design* (light, separable spaces, food variety) and *leader behaviour* (participate, recognise, hold the feast).
- **Act 2:** delete the end date and the sponsor; conflict routes inward; the first death founds a custom; hunger and value drift are the slow burns; the unselected second generation begins; the community's own memory (veterans, rituals) is its only institutional capital.
- **Act 3:** outside authority returns and audits what drifted (seed 17); outside faction fights recruit inside allies again (seed 4); confederation partners become the new displacement targets.

## 4. Open questions

1. **Is the TQP an expectancy artefact or a duration artefact?** Stuster's shorter-is-worse finding [5] and the ship/land split [6] both point to expectancy plus monotony, but no study has run a truly open-ended confinement. The Act 2 assumption (no slump without an end date) is an inference, not a finding.
2. **Group size and pathology are confounded with climate, nation and year** in every Antarctic study [2][8][38]. I could not find a clean crew-size coefficient (Palinkas 2000 included crew size as a covariate but the abstract does not report its sign [49]). The threshold table is built from governance form, not from symptom rates.
3. **Children:** no developmental data at all. The sim must choose a model (peer cohort size, schooling, consent) without evidence beyond Esperanza's existence and Pitcairn's failure.
4. **Selection of the unselected.** Whether trait distributions in a born population should be drawn from population norms (higher neuroticism than screened crews) is a design decision with large downstream effect on incident rates.
5. **How much of "culture" to make heritable.** Rituals and dialect clearly persist within a rotating station via a few veterans; whether they persist across a generation with no outside contact is untested (Tristan suggests strongly yes).
6. **Alcohol in a closed-loop economy.** Every ICE community brews; the sim needs a rule for how much ethanol a habitat's biomass loop can spare and who controls it — an economics question (04) as much as a social one.
7. **Body disposal in a mass-closed habitat** is a design decision (recycle vs preserve vs eject) with no terrestrial precedent beyond burial at sea; it will define the Voidborn as much as any other choice.
